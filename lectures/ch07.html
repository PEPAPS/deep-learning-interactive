Lecture 6 · Chapter 7

Regularization

Deep networks have enormous capacity, so they can memorize their training data. Regularization is the toolbox for making them work on new data: penalties on the weights (L2, L1), more and noisier data (augmentation, noise, label smoothing), stopping at the right time (early stopping), and averaging many models (bagging, dropout).

7 · Regularization

Regularization: trade a little training error for test error

Regularization = any change to the learning algorithm meant to reduce generalization error, not training error. In practice the best model is almost always a large model that has been regularized appropriately.

In plain words

  • Many strategies are explicitly designed to reduce test error, possibly at the cost of higher training error — collectively, regularization.
  • Recall the bias–variance view: a good regularizer trades a small increase in bias for a large reduction in variance.
  • Rather than searching for a model of exactly the right size, take a big network and control its effective capacity.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Unregularized big network: training error 0.02, test error 0.30 (gap 0.28 → overfitting).
  2. Same network with dropout + weight decay: training error 0.05, test error 0.18.
  3. Training error went up by 0.03, test error went down by 0.12 — that is what “regularization” means.

7.1 · Parameter norm penalties

Parameter norm penalties

Add a penalty Ω(θ) on the size of the parameters, weighted by a hyper-parameter α ≥ 0. Penalize the weights, not the biases.

\[ \tilde J(\boldsymbol\theta;\mathbf X,\mathbf y)=J(\boldsymbol\theta;\mathbf X,\mathbf y)+\alpha\,\Omega(\boldsymbol\theta),\qquad\alpha\in[0,\infty) \](7.1)

In plain words

  • α = 0: no regularization; larger α: more regularization. Limits capacity.
  • Penalize only the weights of each layer’s affine transformation, leave biases unregularized:
    • each weight describes how two variables interact → needs data in many conditions to fit;
    • each bias controls a single variable → needs less data, little variance added by leaving it free;
    • regularizing biases can cause significant underfitting.
  • Different α per layer is possible, but searching many hyper-parameters is expensive, so often one α for all layers.

Terms on this slide

\(\tilde J\)
The regularized objective (what we actually minimize).
Ω(θ)
Norm penalty measuring the size of the parameters.
α
Penalty strength (a hyper-parameter tuned on validation data).
w vs. θ
w = the weights that get penalized; θ = all parameters including biases.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Data loss J = 0.40; weights w = (3, −1); bias b = 7 (not penalized).
  2. L2 penalty \(\Omega=\frac12(3^2+(-1)^2)=5\).
  3. With α = 0.01: \(\tilde J=0.40+0.01\times5=0.45\). The large bias adds nothing to the penalty.

7.1.1 · L² parameter regularization

L2 regularization (weight decay)

Each step shrinks the weights by a constant factor before the gradient update. Over the whole training run it shrinks w* along each Hessian eigen-direction by \(\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\alpha)\) — strongly where the loss doesn’t care, hardly where it does.

\[ \tilde J(\mathbf w)=\frac\alpha2\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf w+J(\mathbf w),\qquad\nabla_{\mathbf w}\tilde J=\alpha\mathbf w+\nabla_{\mathbf w}J \](7.2–7.3) \[ \mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\epsilon(\alpha\mathbf w+\nabla_{\mathbf w}J)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbf w\leftarrow(1-\epsilon\alpha)\mathbf w-\epsilon\nabla_{\mathbf w}J \](7.4–7.5) \[ \hat J(\boldsymbol\theta)=J(\mathbf w^*)+\tfrac12(\mathbf w-\mathbf w^*)^{\top}\mathbf H(\mathbf w-\mathbf w^*),\qquad\alpha\tilde{\mathbf w}+\mathbf H(\tilde{\mathbf w}-\mathbf w^*)=0 \](7.6–7.8) \[ \tilde{\mathbf w}=(\mathbf H+\alpha\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf H\mathbf w^*=\mathbf Q(\boldsymbol\Lambda+\alpha\mathbf I)^{-1}\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf Q^{\top}\mathbf w^* \](7.10–7.13)

In plain words

  • Ω = ½‖w‖²₂ drives weights toward the origin. Also called ridge regression or Tikhonov regularization.
  • Approximate J by a quadratic around its unregularized minimum w*: no first-order term (gradient is 0 there), H positive semidefinite.
  • As α → 0, w̃ → w*. As α grows, the component along eigenvector i is multiplied by \(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+\alpha}\): if \(\lambda_i\gg\alpha\) almost unchanged; if \(\lambda_i\ll\alpha\) shrunk to nearly 0.
  • Small λᵢ = moving that way barely changes the loss = an unimportant direction → decayed away. Important directions survive.

Terms on this slide

w*
Minimum of the unregularized training cost.
w̃
Minimum of the regularized cost.
H = QΛQᵀ
Eigendecomposition: Q = orthonormal eigenvectors, Λ = diagonal eigenvalues.
Positive semidefinite
All eigenvalues ≥ 0 (true at a minimum).

Calculation, step by step

  1. One step: w = 2, ∇J = 0.5, ε = 0.1, α = 0.5 → \(w\leftarrow(1-0.05)\cdot2-0.1\cdot0.5=1.9-0.05=1.85\).
  2. Whole-run effect: eigenvalues λ₁ = 0.1, λ₂ = 10, α = 1 → factors \(0.1/1.1=0.091\) and \(10/11=0.909\).
  3. If \(\mathbf w^*=(2,2)\) in the eigenbasis: \(\tilde{\mathbf w}=(0.18,\,1.82)\) — the unimportant direction almost vanishes.

7.1.1 · L2 for linear regression

L2 in linear regression: the input “looks” noisier

L2 changes the normal equations from \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf y\) to \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X+\alpha\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf y\): α is added to the variance of every feature, so weights on features weakly related to y shrink most.

\[ (\mathbf X\mathbf w-\mathbf y)^{\top}(\mathbf X\mathbf w-\mathbf y)\ \to\ (\mathbf X\mathbf w-\mathbf y)^{\top}(\mathbf X\mathbf w-\mathbf y)+\tfrac12\alpha\,\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf w \](7.14–7.15) \[ \mathbf w=(\mathbf X^{\top}\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\top}\mathbf y\ \to\ \mathbf w=(\mathbf X^{\top}\mathbf X+\alpha\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\top}\mathbf y \](7.16–7.17)

In plain words

  • \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\) is proportional to the covariance matrix \(\frac1m\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\); its diagonal holds each feature’s variance.
  • L2 adds α to that diagonal → the algorithm “perceives” every input as having higher variance.
  • So it shrinks weights on features whose covariance with the target is low compared to that added variance.

Terms on this slide

Covariance matrix
How features vary and co-vary; diagonal = variances.
Ridge path
How each weight changes as α goes from 0 to ∞.

Calculation, step by step

  1. \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf y=(8,1)\) → unregularized w = (8/4, 1/1) = (2, 1).
  2. α = 1: \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X+\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf y=(8/5,\ 1/2)=(1.6,\ 0.5)\).
  3. The high-variance feature’s weight drops 20%; the low-variance one is halved.
7.1.2 · L¹ regularization

L1 regularization: sparse weights

The L1 penalty’s gradient is a constant push α·sign(w), not proportional to w. Result: a soft threshold — small weights become exactly zero, which selects features.

\[ \Omega(\boldsymbol\theta)=\|\mathbf w\|_1=\sum_i|w_i|,\qquad\tilde J=\alpha\|\mathbf w\|_1+J,\qquad\nabla_{\mathbf w}\tilde J=\alpha\,\text{sign}(\mathbf w)+\nabla_{\mathbf w}J \](7.18–7.20) \[ \hat J(\mathbf w)=J(\mathbf w^*)+\sum_i\Big[\tfrac12H_{i,i}(w_i-w_i^*)^2+\alpha|w_i|\Big] \](7.22) diagonal Hessian \[ w_i=\text{sign}(w_i^*)\max\Big\{|w_i^*|-\frac{\alpha}{H_{i,i}},\,0\Big\} \](7.23)

In plain words

  • sign(w) is applied element-wise; at 0 we use a sub-gradient. No clean algebraic solution for a general Hessian, so assume a diagonal one (e.g. inputs decorrelated with PCA).
  • For \(w_i^*>0\): if \(w_i^*\le\alpha/H_{i,i}\) → \(w_i=0\) (penalty overwhelms the data); otherwise it is shifted toward 0 by \(\alpha/H_{i,i}\). Same for negative weights.
  • Sparse = many parameters with optimal value 0. Used for feature selection; the LASSO = linear model + least squares + L1.

Terms on this slide

‖w‖₁
Sum of absolute values.
Sub-gradient
A generalized slope for functions with a kink (like |w| at 0).
Soft threshold
Move toward 0 by a fixed amount; stop at 0.
LASSO
Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator.

Calculation, step by step

  1. w* = (2, 0.3, −0.8), H = I, α = 0.5.
  2. L1: \(2-0.5=1.5\); \(|0.3|\le0.5\Rightarrow0\); \(-0.8\to-(0.8-0.5)=-0.3\) → (1.5, 0, −0.3).
  3. L2 with the same α: divide by 1.5 → (1.33, 0.2, −0.53) — nothing is exactly 0.

Connections

  • Builds onMAP estimation (L2): L1 corresponds to a Laplace prior on the weights.
7.4 · Dataset augmentation

Dataset augmentation: more (fake) data

The best way to generalize is more data. For classification we can create new (x, y) pairs by transforming x in ways that should not change y — as long as the transformation really keeps the label.

In plain words

  • A classifier must be invariant to many transformations, so generating transformed copies is easy and effective — especially for object recognition: shifts by a few pixels, rotations, scaling.
  • Helps even if the model is already partly translation invariant (e.g. a CNN).
  • Harder for other tasks: generating fake data for density estimation requires already solving density estimation.
  • Don’t change the class: OCR must distinguish b/d and 6/9, so no horizontal flips or 180° rotations.
  • Noise injection into inputs (and hidden units — augmentation at many levels of abstraction) is also augmentation; dropout constructs new inputs by multiplying by noise.

Terms on this slide

Invariance
The output doesn’t change when the input is transformed.
OCR
Optical character recognition (reading text from images).

Calculation, step by step

  1. 60 000 training digits, each shifted to 8 neighbouring positions (±1 px) → \(60\,000\times9=540\,000\) examples.
  2. Add 2 small rotations each → \(540\,000\times3=1\,620\,000\) — 27× more data at almost no cost.

Connections

  • Leads toEquivariance & pooling in CNNs (L10): building the invariance into the architecture instead of the data.
7.5 · Noise robustness

Noise robustness: inputs, hidden units and weights

Tiny input noise ≈ a weight-norm penalty. Noise on the weights pushes the model toward flat minima — places where small parameter changes barely change the output.

\[ \boldsymbol\epsilon_{\mathbf W}\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\epsilon;\mathbf 0,\eta\mathbf I),\qquad\tilde J_{\mathbf W}=\mathbb E_{p(\mathbf x,y,\boldsymbol\epsilon_{\mathbf W})}\Big[\big(\hat y_{\boldsymbol\epsilon_{\mathbf W}}(\mathbf x)-y\big)^2\Big] \](7.31) \[ \tilde J_{\mathbf W}\approx J+\eta\,\mathbb E_{p(\mathbf x,y)}\Big[\big\|\nabla_{\mathbf W}\hat y(\mathbf x)\big\|^2\Big]\quad\text{(small }\eta) \](7.33)

In plain words

  • For some models, input noise with infinitesimal variance = a penalty on the norm of the weights. In general noise is more powerful than shrinking parameters, especially on hidden units.
  • Weight noise (mainly used for RNNs): each presentation of an input uses randomly perturbed weights. The extra term penalizes outputs that are sensitive to the weights.
  • It finds points that are not merely minima, but minima surrounded by flat regions.

Terms on this slide

η
Variance of the weight noise.
\(\hat y_{\boldsymbol\epsilon_{\mathbf W}}\)
Output of the network with perturbed weights W + ε.
Flat minimum
A minimum where the loss stays low over a wide region of weights.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Second-order view: \(\mathbb E[J(w+\epsilon)]\approx J(w)+\frac12\sigma^2J''(w)\).
  2. Sharp minimum: J = 0.0, J″ = 100. Flat minimum: J = 0.2, J″ = 1. Noise σ = 0.1.
  3. Sharp: \(0+\frac12(0.01)(100)=0.5\). Flat: \(0.2+\frac12(0.01)(1)=0.205\) → the flat minimum wins under noise.

7.5.1 · Injecting noise at the output targets

Label smoothing

Labels contain mistakes, and a softmax can never output exactly 0 or 1. Replace the hard targets with 1 − ε (correct) and ε/(k − 1) (others): the model stops chasing infinite confidence but still learns to classify correctly.

\[ \text{target}_i=\begin{cases}1-\epsilon&i=\text{label}\\ \dfrac{\epsilon}{k-1}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\text{loss}=-\sum_i\text{target}_i\log\text{softmax}(\mathbf z)_i \]

In plain words

  • Maximizing log p(y|x) is harmful if y is wrong. Model the noise: assume the label is correct with probability 1 − ε, otherwise any other label could be correct.
  • This can be built into the cost analytically (no sampling): use soft targets with the usual cross-entropy.
  • With hard targets, maximum likelihood may never converge: the softmax can’t hit 0/1, so the weights grow larger and larger, making more extreme predictions forever.
  • Weight decay could stop that too, but label smoothing prevents hard probabilities without discouraging correct classification.

Terms on this slide

Hard target
One-hot vector (1 for the label, 0 elsewhere).
Soft target
A probability vector that spreads a little mass ε over the other classes.
Margin d
How much larger the correct logit is than the others.

Calculation, step by step

  1. k = 10, ε = 0.1: correct target 0.9, each other \(0.1/9=0.0111\).
  2. Best margin: \(d^*=\log\frac{(1-\epsilon)(k-1)}{\epsilon}=\log\frac{0.9\times9}{0.1}=\log81=4.39\).
  3. With hard targets there is no best d: the loss keeps shrinking as d → ∞.

7.8 · Early stopping

Early stopping

Training error keeps falling, validation error eventually rises. Save the parameters whenever validation improves; stop after p checks without improvement; return the saved parameters. Simple, effective, the most common regularizer in deep learning.

In plain words

  • View it as an efficient hyper-parameter search: the number of training steps has a U-shaped validation curve.
  • It controls effective capacity by limiting how many steps the model gets to fit the training set.
  • Costs: evaluating on the validation set periodically (ideally on a separate machine/GPU) and storing a copy of the best parameters (cheap; written rarely, never read during training).
  • Barely changes the training procedure, the objective or the allowed parameters — unlike weight decay, where too much can trap the network in a bad local minimum. No penalty term, no extra gradients.
  • It needs a validation set, i.e. some data isn’t used for training. Afterwards: (1) retrain from scratch on all data for the same number of steps (or epochs?), or (2) continue training on all data until the validation loss falls below the training loss at the stopping point (cheaper, but may never terminate).

Terms on this slide

Patience p
How many evaluations without improvement before stopping.
Validation check
Evaluating the current parameters on the validation set.
Effective capacity
What the model can actually reach given the training procedure.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Validation errors at successive checks: 0.30, 0.25, 0.22, 0.23, 0.24, 0.26. Patience p = 3.
  2. Best so far is 0.22 at check 3 → save θ. Checks 4, 5, 6 don’t improve → 3 in a row → stop after check 6.
  3. Return the θ saved at check 3, not the latest one.

7.8 · Early stopping as regularization

Why early stopping behaves like weight decay

Starting from w = 0, gradient descent fits high-curvature directions quickly and low-curvature ones slowly. Stopping after τ steps therefore shrinks the same directions as L2 with \(\alpha\approx\frac{1}{\tau\epsilon}\).

\[ \mathbf w^{(\tau)}=\mathbf w^{(\tau-1)}-\epsilon\mathbf H(\mathbf w^{(\tau-1)}-\mathbf w^*)\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf w^{(\tau)}-\mathbf w^*=(\mathbf I-\epsilon\mathbf H)(\mathbf w^{(\tau-1)}-\mathbf w^*) \](7.35–7.36) \[ \mathbf Q^{\top}\mathbf w^{(\tau)}=\big[\mathbf I-(\mathbf I-\epsilon\boldsymbol\Lambda)^{\tau}\big]\mathbf Q^{\top}\mathbf w^*\qquad\mathbf Q^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf w}=\big[\mathbf I-(\boldsymbol\Lambda+\alpha\mathbf I)^{-1}\alpha\big]\mathbf Q^{\top}\mathbf w^* \](7.40), (7.42) \[ (\mathbf I-\epsilon\boldsymbol\Lambda)^{\tau}=(\boldsymbol\Lambda+\alpha\mathbf I)^{-1}\alpha\quad\Rightarrow\quad\tau\approx\frac{1}{\epsilon\alpha},\qquad\alpha\approx\frac{1}{\tau\epsilon} \](7.43–7.45)

In plain words

  • ε·τ (learning rate × steps) measures effective capacity: with bounded gradients, it limits how far from θ₀ the parameters can travel — like the reciprocal of a weight-decay coefficient.
  • Assumptions: linear model, quadratic loss, plain gradient descent, w⁽⁰⁾ = 0, ε small enough that \(|1-\epsilon\lambda_i|<1\).
  • Taking logs and using \(\log(1+x)\approx x\) for small ελᵢ and λᵢ/α gives τ ≈ 1/(εα).
  • Early stopping is more than restricting trajectory length: it watches validation error and stops at a good point, so it chooses the amount of regularization automatically, while weight decay needs many training runs with different α.

Terms on this slide

τ
Number of gradient steps taken.
(I − εΛ)^τ
How much of each eigen-component of the initial error remains after τ steps.

Calculation, step by step

  1. ε = 0.01, τ = 1 000 → equivalent α ≈ 1/(1000 × 0.01) = 0.1.
  2. Direction with λ = 0.05: early stopping keeps \(1-(1-0.0005)^{1000}=1-0.606=0.394\); L2 keeps \(0.05/(0.05+0.1)=0.333\) — similar.
  3. Direction with λ = 5: early stopping keeps \(1-(0.95)^{1000}\approx1.0\); L2 keeps \(5/5.1=0.98\).
7.11 · Bagging and other ensemble methods

Bagging and model averaging

Train several models and average their votes. Because different models make partly independent errors, the average is better than a typical member — up to k× better if errors are independent.

\[ \mathbb E\Big[\Big(\frac1k\sum_i\epsilon_i\Big)^2\Big]=\frac{1}{k^2}\mathbb E\Big[\sum_i\Big(\epsilon_i^2+\sum_{j\ne i}\epsilon_i\epsilon_j\Big)\Big]=\frac1k v+\frac{k-1}{k}c \](7.50)

In plain words

  • Bagging = bootstrap aggregating. A case of model averaging; techniques using it are ensemble methods.
  • Errors εᵢ with variance v and covariance c: perfectly correlated (c = v) → MSE = v, no help; uncorrelated (c = 0) → MSE = v/k. The ensemble is never worse on average than its members.
  • Bagging: build k datasets of the original size by sampling with replacement → each misses some examples and duplicates others → model i trained on dataset i.
  • Neural nets differ enough even on the same data (random init, minibatch order, hyper-parameters, non-determinism) to benefit from averaging. ML contests are usually won by ensembles of dozens of models.
  • Boosting is different: it builds an ensemble with higher capacity (adding networks, or hidden units, incrementally).

Terms on this slide

Bootstrap sample
A dataset of size m drawn with replacement from the original m examples.
v, c
Variance of each model’s error, covariance between two models’ errors.
Ensemble
A set of models whose predictions are combined.

Calculation, step by step

  1. k = 10 models, v = 1, c = 0.3: \(\frac{1}{10}+\frac{9}{10}\cdot0.3=0.1+0.27=0.37\).
  2. So the averaged model has 63% lower expected squared error than one model.
  3. With c = 0 it would be 0.1; with c = 1 it stays 1.0.

7.12 · Dropout

Dropout: bagging exponentially many networks, cheaply

For every example, randomly remove input and hidden units (multiply their output by 0). This trains an ensemble of all sub-networks that share parameters — exponentially many models with the memory of one.

\[ \min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\mu}J(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\mu)\qquad\mu_i\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p_i),\ \ p_{\text{input}}\approx0.8,\ \ p_{\text{hidden}}\approx0.5 \]

In plain words

  • Bagging big networks is impractical (train/evaluate k of them). Dropout approximates it over all sub-networks formed by removing non-output units.
  • Each time an example is loaded, sample an independent binary mask μ for all input and hidden units (inclusion probability is a hyper-parameter); then run forward prop, backprop and the update as usual.
  • The expectation has exponentially many terms, but sampling μ gives an unbiased estimate of its gradient.
  • Differences from bagging: models share parameters (tractable memory), and most sub-networks are never trained at all — a tiny fraction is trained for one step each, and sharing makes the rest good too.

Terms on this slide

Mask μ
Vector of 0/1 values: 1 = keep the unit, 0 = drop it.
Inclusion probability p
Chance that a unit is kept.
Sub-network
The network with some units removed.
Parameter sharing
All sub-networks use the same underlying weights.

Calculation, step by step

  1. MLP with 784 inputs and two hidden layers of 1 000: droppable units = 2 784 → \(2^{2784}\) sub-networks.
  2. Expected kept units per example: \(0.8\times784+0.5\times2000\approx1627\).
  3. Probability of one specific mask for 4 inputs (p = 0.8) where we keep 3: \(0.8^3\times0.2=0.1024\).

7.12 · Dropout (inference and analysis)

Predicting with dropout: the weight-scaling rule

The ensemble’s prediction would need an average over exponentially many masks. Instead, run the full network once with each unit’s outgoing weights multiplied by its inclusion probability — exact for softmax regression, a very good approximation for deep nets.

\[ \text{bagging: }\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{(i)}(y\mid\mathbf x)\qquad\text{dropout: }\sum_{\boldsymbol\mu}p(\boldsymbol\mu)\,p(y\mid\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\mu) \](7.52–7.53) \[ \tilde p_{\text{ensemble}}(y\mid\mathbf x)=\sqrt[2^d]{\prod_{\boldsymbol\mu}p(y\mid\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\mu)},\qquad p_{\text{ensemble}}(y\mid\mathbf x)=\frac{\tilde p_{\text{ensemble}}(y\mid\mathbf x)}{\sum_{y'}\tilde p_{\text{ensemble}}(y'\mid\mathbf x)} \](7.54–7.55) \[ P(y\mid\mathbf v;\mathbf d)=\text{softmax}\big(\mathbf W^{\top}(\mathbf d\odot\mathbf v)+\mathbf b\big)_y\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ P_{\text{ensemble}}(y\mid\mathbf v)=\text{softmax}\Big(\tfrac12\mathbf W^{\top}\mathbf v+\mathbf b\Big)_y \](7.56–7.66)

In plain words

  • Inference = combining votes. Approximations: average 10–20 sampled masks, or the cheaper weight scaling inference rule (one forward pass) that matches the geometric mean.
  • Motivation: keep the expected total input to each unit at test time equal to training time. With p = ½: divide weights by 2 after training — or multiply unit states by 2 during training.
  • Exact for softmax regression, conditionally Gaussian regression, and deep nets without non-linearities; approximate otherwise, but works well (sometimes better than 1 000-sample Monte Carlo; sometimes 20 samples win — problem dependent). Dropout beat weight decay in experiments.
  • Cost: O(n) per example per update to sample and apply n binary numbers (+ O(n) memory until backprop). Inference costs the same as without dropout.
  • Works with nearly any model trained by SGD with distributed representations (MLPs, RBMs, RNNs). Reduces effective capacity → use bigger models and train longer. Less useful with huge datasets or very few labels.
  • Why it works: hidden units must be good in many contexts (any partner may be missing); it destroys extracted features (e.g. the “nose” detector) rather than raw pixels; and the noise is multiplicative, so units can’t just grow large to drown additive noise. Batch norm’s noise has a similar regularizing effect.

Terms on this slide

Arithmetic mean
Average of the probabilities.
Geometric mean
2ᵈ-th root of the product of the probabilities (then renormalized).
Weight scaling rule
Multiply outgoing weights by p at test time.
Monte Carlo approximation
Average predictions over a few random masks.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Hidden unit with output h = 4, kept with p = 0.5 during training: expected contribution to the next layer = 0.5 × 4 × w = 2w.
  2. At test time it is always present, so scale: (4 × w) × 0.5 = 2w — same expectation.
  3. “Inverted dropout” alternative: during training multiply kept units by 1/p = 2 → 8w half the time, 0 otherwise → expectation 4w; then use the network unchanged at test time.

Summary: how it all connects

All regularizers reduce variance at a small cost in bias: penalize weights (L2/L1), enrich data (augmentation, noise, smoothing), stop early (≈ L2), and average models (bagging, dropout).
