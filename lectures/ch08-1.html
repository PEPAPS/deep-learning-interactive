Optimization I: Why Training Is Hard

Training a network is not the same as minimizing a function: we optimize a stand-in (empirical risk, surrogate losses) with noisy minibatch gradients and stop early. And the landscape itself is hard: ill-conditioning, local minima, saddle points, cliffs, vanishing/exploding gradients, and local directions that point the wrong way.

8.1.1 · Empirical risk minimization

Risk vs. empirical risk

We want low risk (expected loss on the true distribution) but can only compute the empirical risk (average loss on the training set). Minimizing the latter and hoping the former drops is empirical risk minimization — and it can overfit.

\[ J^*(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbb E_{(\mathbf x,y)\sim p_{\text{data}}}L\big(f(\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\theta),y\big) \](8.1) risk \[ \mathbb E_{\mathbf x,y\sim\hat p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf x,y)}\big[L(f(\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\theta),y)\big]=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}L\big(f(\mathbf x^{(i)};\boldsymbol\theta),y^{(i)}\big) \](8.2) empirical risk

In plain words

  • If we knew \(p_{\text{data}}\), minimizing risk would be pure optimization. Knowing only samples makes it a machine learning problem.
  • Replace the true distribution by the empirical one \(\hat p_{\text{data}}\) (the training set) → empirical risk minimization.
  • Problems: high-capacity models can memorize the training set, and useful losses like the 0-1 loss have no useful derivatives. So in deep learning we rarely do pure ERM — we optimize something even further from what we truly want.

Terms on this slide

Risk J*
Expected loss under the true data distribution (= generalization error).
Empirical risk
Average loss over the m training examples.
\(\hat p_{\text{data}}\)
Empirical distribution: probability 1/m on each training example.
0-1 loss
1 for a wrong class, 0 for a right one.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Three training losses 0.2, 0.5, 0.8 → empirical risk \((0.2+0.5+0.8)/3=0.5\).
  2. On 10 000 fresh examples the average loss is 0.65 — an estimate of the risk; the 0.15 gap is overfitting.
  3. In the demo: \(J^*(\theta)=(\theta-1.5)^2/3+0.25\); with m = 8 points the empirical minimizer might be θ̂ = 1.8 → true risk \(0.09/3+0.25=0.28\).

8.1.2 · Surrogate loss functions and early stopping

Surrogate losses — and stopping before a minimum

When the loss we care about (0-1 error) can’t be optimized, we optimize a surrogate (negative log-likelihood). It can even learn more. And training stops by early stopping on the true loss — usually while gradients are still large.

In plain words

  • Exactly minimizing expected 0-1 loss is typically intractable (exponential in input dimension), even for a linear classifier.
  • The NLL of the correct class is the usual surrogate: it estimates class probabilities; with good probabilities we pick the classes with least expected error.
  • Surprise: the test 0-1 loss often keeps decreasing long after the training 0-1 loss hits zero — the NLL keeps pushing the classes apart, making the classifier more confident and robust.
  • Pure optimization stops when the gradient is ≈ 0. Training stops when an early-stopping criterion (based on the true loss, e.g. validation 0-1 error) is met — often with large derivatives remaining.

Terms on this slide

Surrogate loss
A loss that is easy to optimize and acts as a proxy for the one we care about.
Margin
(2y − 1)·z: how far on the correct side of the boundary an example is.
Convergence criterion
The rule that decides when to stop training.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Example with margin 2 (correctly classified): 0-1 loss = 0, NLL = \(\log(1+e^{-2})=0.127\).
  2. Margin 4: 0-1 loss still 0, NLL = \(\log(1+e^{-4})=0.018\) — NLL still rewards moving further.
  3. Derivative of the 0-1 loss w.r.t. the margin: 0 at both points → no learning signal; NLL derivative \(-\sigma(-m)\): −0.119 and −0.018.

8.1.3 · Batch and minibatch algorithms

Batch, stochastic and minibatch methods

The objective is a sum over examples, so its gradient is an expectation we can estimate from a random sample. Small random minibatches give fast, slightly noisy updates — usually far more efficient than exact gradients.

\[ \boldsymbol\theta_{\text{ML}}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p_{\text{model}}\big(\mathbf x^{(i)},y^{(i)};\boldsymbol\theta\big)=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ \mathbb E_{\mathbf x,y\sim\hat p_{\text{data}}}\log p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf x,y;\boldsymbol\theta) \](8.3–8.4) \[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbb E_{\mathbf x,y\sim\hat p_{\text{data}}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\log p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf x,y;\boldsymbol\theta),\qquad\text{SE}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \](8.5) \[ \hat{\mathbf g}=\frac1m\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\sum_iL\big(f(\mathbf x^{(i)};\boldsymbol\theta),y^{(i)}\big)\ \text{ with fresh samples from }p_{\text{data}}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{unbiased for }\nabla J^* \](8.7–8.8)

In plain words

  • Less than linear returns: n = 100 vs 10 000 examples → 100× compute, only 10× smaller standard error. Fast approximate gradients converge faster in total computation.
  • Redundancy: many examples contribute nearly the same gradient (worst case: all identical — one sample would suffice).
  • Names: batch / deterministic = whole training set; stochastic / online = one example (online = from a stream); minibatch (now just “stochastic”) = in between. “Batch size” usually means minibatch size.
  • Choosing the size: bigger = more accurate but diminishing returns; multicore hardware is underused below some minimum; memory grows with batch size; GPUs like powers of 2 (32–256, 16 for big models); small batches regularize (best generalization often at size 1) but need small learning rates and many steps.
  • Gradient-only methods are robust with ~100 examples; second-order methods need ~10 000 because multiplying by \(\mathbf H^{-1}\) amplifies errors.
  • Randomness matters: examples within and across minibatches should be independent → shuffle (once, then store shuffled, is usually enough).
  • Each minibatch of new data gives an unbiased gradient of the generalization error; only the first epoch does this exactly, but extra epochs still help (lower training error). With huge datasets, one pass (or less) is common — underfitting and compute dominate.

Terms on this slide

Epoch
One full pass over the training set.
Standard error σ/√n
Typical error of an average of n samples.
Shuffle
Randomize the order of examples so consecutive ones are independent.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Per-example gradient std σ = 2. Batch 100: SE = 2/10 = 0.2. Batch 10 000: SE = 2/100 = 0.02.
  2. 50 000 training examples with batch 128: \(\lceil50000/128\rceil=391\) updates per epoch.
  3. Same budget with full-batch GD: 1 update per epoch — 391× fewer steps.

8.2.1 · Ill-conditioning

Ill-conditioning

Even convex problems suffer when the Hessian is ill-conditioned: the step must be tiny to avoid going uphill along steep directions, so learning is slow despite a strong gradient.

\[ \text{change in cost after a step }-\epsilon\mathbf g:\qquad\tfrac12\epsilon^2\,\mathbf g^{\top}\mathbf H\mathbf g-\epsilon\,\mathbf g^{\top}\mathbf g \](8.10)

In plain words

  • Machine learning used to avoid general optimization by designing convex problems. Neural nets force us into the general non-convex case — but some problems exist even for convex functions.
  • Ill-conditioning becomes a problem when \(\frac12\epsilon^2\mathbf g^\top\mathbf H\mathbf g>\epsilon\mathbf g^\top\mathbf g\). SGD then looks “stuck”: even very small steps increase the cost.
  • Observed in real networks: the gradient norm \(\mathbf g^\top\mathbf g\) doesn’t shrink much during training, but \(\mathbf g^\top\mathbf H\mathbf g\) grows by over an order of magnitude.
  • Newton’s method is the classic fix for ill-conditioned convex problems, but it needs big changes before it works for neural networks (Lecture 9).

Terms on this slide

Ill-conditioned
Hessian eigenvalues differ hugely (large condition number).
gᵀg
Squared gradient length: the first-order gain per unit step.
gᵀHg
Curvature along the gradient direction: the second-order penalty.

Calculation, step by step

  1. gᵀg = 4, gᵀHg = 400, ε = 0.05.
  2. Gain \(\epsilon\mathbf g^\top\mathbf g=0.2\); penalty \(\frac12(0.05)^2(400)=0.5\) → cost changes by +0.3: uphill.
  3. Need \(\epsilon<2\mathbf g^\top\mathbf g/\mathbf g^\top\mathbf H\mathbf g=8/400=0.02\) just to go downhill — tiny steps.

8.2.2 · Local minima

Local minima: many, but mostly harmless

Neural nets have astronomically many local minima — but most come from symmetries (equivalent networks with identical cost). Local minima only hurt if their cost is much higher than the global minimum, and for large nets that seems rare.

In plain words

  • Convex: any local minimum is global (maybe a flat bottom region — any point there is fine). Non-convex nets: essentially guaranteed to have an extremely large number of local minima.
  • Model identifiability: a model is identifiable if enough data rules out all but one parameter setting. Nets aren’t:
    • Weight-space symmetry: swap two hidden units (incoming and outgoing weights) → same function; m layers of n units → \(n!^m\) arrangements.
    • ReLU / maxout: scale a unit’s incoming weights and bias by α and its outgoing weights by 1/α → same function; every minimum lies on an (m×n)-dimensional hyperbola of equivalent minima (unless weight decay breaks it).
  • These minima have equal cost → not a problematic non-convexity.
  • Experts now suspect that for sufficiently large networks most local minima have low cost; finding a low-but-not-minimal point is good enough.

Terms on this slide

Identifiable
Enough data pins down a single parameter setting.
Latent variables
Hidden quantities (here: hidden units) that can be relabelled.
n!
n factorial: number of ways to order n items.

Calculation, step by step

  1. 2 hidden layers × 4 units: \((4!)^2=24^2=576\) equivalent arrangements of every solution.
  2. 2 layers × 100 units: \((100!)^2\approx10^{316}\).
  3. ReLU scaling: unit input z = 2. Multiply incoming weights by α = 3 → z = 6, ReLU = 6; outgoing weight ÷ 3 → contribution 6/3 = 2: unchanged.

8.2.3 · Plateaus, saddle points and other flat regions

Saddle points and flat regions

In high dimensions, critical points are mostly saddle points, not minima: being a minimum needs every eigenvalue positive, which is exponentially unlikely. Gradient descent usually escapes saddles; Newton’s method is attracted to them.

In plain words

  • At a saddle the Hessian has positive and negative eigenvalues: a minimum along one cross-section, a maximum along another.
  • For many random functions of n variables: local minima common in low dimensions, rare in high dimensions; the ratio of saddles to minima grows exponentially with n.
  • Hessian eigenvalues become more likely positive at lower cost → local minima tend to have low cost; high-cost critical points are mostly saddles; extremely high-cost ones tend to be maxima.
  • Gradient-only methods: the gradient can get very small near a saddle, but empirically gradient descent escapes in many cases.
  • Newton’s method solves for gradient = 0 and can jump to a saddle — one reason second-order methods haven’t replaced gradient descent for neural nets.
  • Wide flat regions (plateaus) with zero gradient and Hessian are also hard for all methods.

Terms on this slide

Saddle point
Critical point with mixed-sign Hessian eigenvalues.
Plateau
A wide region where the loss is nearly constant.
Random function
A mathematical model of a “typical” high-dimensional landscape.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Coin-flip model: each of n eigenvalue signs is + with probability ½.
  2. P(minimum) = P(all +) = \(2^{-n}\): n = 2 → 0.25; n = 10 → 0.001; n = 1 000 → \(10^{-301}\).
  3. So a random critical point in 10 dimensions is a saddle ~99.8% of the time (excluding maxima, also \(2^{-n}\)).

8.2.4 · Cliffs and exploding gradients

Cliffs and exploding gradients

Multiplying several large weights creates cliffs: extremely steep walls where one gradient step jumps the parameters far away. Gradient clipping caps the step so we stay where the gradient is meaningful.

\[ \text{if }\|\mathbf g\|>v:\quad\mathbf g\leftarrow\frac{\mathbf g\,v}{\|\mathbf g\|} \]norm clipping (details in L11)

In plain words

  • Deep networks often have extremely steep regions resembling cliffs, from the multiplication of several large weights.
  • On a cliff face the gradient update can move the parameters extremely far, usually jumping off the structure — dangerous approached from above or below.
  • The gradient gives a direction only locally; when gradient descent proposes a huge step, clipping reduces it so we don’t leave the region where the gradient is approximately the steepest-descent direction.
  • Most common in recurrent networks: one multiplication per time step → long sequences = extreme multiplication.

Terms on this slide

Cliff
A region where the loss changes extremely fast.
Exploding gradient
A gradient so large that one step is catastrophic.
Clipping threshold v
Maximum allowed gradient norm.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Five weights of 3 multiplied together: \(3^5=243\) — small changes are amplified hugely.
  2. Gradient g = (300, 400), ‖g‖ = 500, threshold v = 5 → g ← (300, 400) × 5/500 = (3, 4).
  3. Same direction, length 5 instead of 500: with ε = 0.1 the step is 0.5 instead of 50.

8.2.5 · Long-term dependencies

Long-term dependencies: vanishing and exploding gradients

A path that multiplies by the same matrix W again and again scales each eigen-direction by λᵗ. Eigenvalues below 1 make gradients vanish; above 1 make them explode.

\[ \mathbf W^{t}=\big(\mathbf V\,\text{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)\,\mathbf V^{-1}\big)^{t}=\mathbf V\,\text{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)^{t}\,\mathbf V^{-1} \](8.11)

In plain words

  • Extremely deep computational graphs: deep feedforward nets, and especially recurrent nets that apply the same parameters at every step.
  • With W’s eigendecomposition, the middle factors \(\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf V\) cancel, leaving \(\text{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)^t\).
  • Vanishing: impossible to know which direction improves the cost. Exploding: learning becomes unstable — the cliffs of the previous slide.

Terms on this slide

Eigendecomposition
W = V diag(λ) V⁻¹: directions V that W only stretches, by factors λ.
Vanishing gradient
Gradient shrinks exponentially with depth/time.
Exploding gradient
Gradient grows exponentially with depth/time.

Calculation, step by step

  1. λ = 0.9 after 50 steps: \(0.9^{50}=0.0052\) — the signal from 50 steps ago is 200× weaker.
  2. λ = 1.1 after 50 steps: \(1.1^{50}=117\) — it is 117× stronger.
  3. Only |λ| ≈ 1 keeps information over long ranges: \(1.0^{50}=1\).

8.2.6 · Inexact gradients

Inexact gradients

Optimization theory assumes exact gradients; in practice we only have noisy (minibatch) or even biased estimates — and sometimes the objective itself is intractable.

In plain words

  • Most algorithms are designed assuming the exact gradient or Hessian; we usually have a noisy or biased estimate.
  • Sometimes the objective we want is intractable — then its gradient is too, and we can only approximate it.
  • Remedies: algorithms designed to tolerate imperfect gradients, or a surrogate loss that is easier to approximate.
  • Noise (variance) makes the iterates wander around the solution; bias makes them converge to the wrong point.

Terms on this slide

Noisy (unbiased) estimate
Right on average, wrong each time.
Biased estimate
Systematically off in one direction.
Intractable objective
Too expensive to compute exactly (e.g. some probabilistic models).

Calculation, step by step

  1. Minimize \(f(x)=\frac12x^2\) (true gradient x) with a biased estimate \(x-0.5\).
  2. Iterations stop where the estimate is 0: \(x-0.5=0\Rightarrow x=0.5\), not the true minimum 0.
  3. With unbiased noise of std 1 and step ε = 0.1, iterates jitter around 0 with spread ≈ \(\sqrt{\epsilon/2}\approx0.22\).

8.2.7 · Poor correspondence between local and global structure

When the local slope points the wrong way

Even with no local minima or saddles, training can fail if the locally best direction does not lead toward distant regions of low cost. Much of training time is the length of the trajectory. Starting in a well-behaved region is the best cure.

In plain words

  • Everything so far was about properties at a single point; you can fix all of them and still fail.
  • In practice, neural nets don’t arrive at a critical point of any kind — such points may not even exist. Softmax NLL can only approach 0; a Gaussian model with learned precision β has NLL → −∞ if the mean is perfect.
  • Being initialized on the wrong side of a “mountain”: in high dimensions you can usually go around it, but the trajectory may be long.
  • Even with a good path, we may not follow it: gradients are approximate (bias/variance); poor conditioning or discontinuities make the region where the gradient is trustworthy tiny, so steps must be small → many steps.
  • Sometimes local information gives no guide (wide flat regions, or landing exactly on a critical point with Newton), or is too greedy (downhill but away from any solution).
  • All might be avoided by initializing in a region connected directly to a solution by a path local descent can follow → research on good initial points.

Terms on this slide

Global structure
The overall shape of the landscape, far from the current point.
Trajectory length
Total distance the parameters travel during training.
Precision β
1/variance of a Gaussian output.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Softmax NLL with margin d between correct and wrong logit: \(\log(1+e^{-d})\): d = 5 → 0.0067; d = 10 → 0.000045; never exactly 0 → no minimum, the weights keep growing.
  2. Gaussian NLL \(\frac12\beta(y-\hat y)^2-\frac12\log\beta+c\): if \(\hat y=y\) exactly, it is \(-\frac12\log\beta\to-\infty\) as β grows.

8.2.8 · Theoretical limits of optimization

Theoretical limits of optimization

Theorems show that some network-training problems are intractable — but they have little bearing on practice: real units are smooth, and a larger network makes acceptable solutions much easier to find.

In plain words

  • Some results only apply to units with discrete outputs; most real units are smooth, so local search works.
  • Some show intractable problem classes exist — but it’s hard to tell if a given problem is in one.
  • Some show finding a solution for a network of a given size is intractable — yet a larger network, where many more parameter settings are acceptable, often finds one easily.
  • We don’t need the exact minimum — just a value low enough to generalize well. Analysing whether an algorithm achieves that is very hard; realistic bounds remain an open research goal.

Calculation, step by step

  1. XOR with exactly 2 hidden ReLU units: the earlier demo (L4) solved it in only ~3 of 10 random starts.
  2. With 8 hidden units, almost every random start reaches zero error: many more parameter settings are solutions.
  3. Over-parametrizing makes optimization easier, and regularization (L6) controls the extra capacity.
