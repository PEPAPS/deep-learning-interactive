The big picture: where every idea plugs in

Training a network means repeating the same loop thousands of times. Everything in this lecture improves one or more steps of this loop. Keep this map in mind: every later slide shows its position on it.

This lecture is about the update step (Part A) and about everything around the optimizer that makes the update easier (Part B).

Part A changes step 6

Newton, conjugate gradients, BFGS and L-BFGS are different rules for “given the gradient, where do I move?” They use (approximate) curvature to choose both the direction and the step length.

Part B changes everything else

Batch norm changes the model, pretraining changes the start, continuation changes the objective, curriculum changes the data, and Polyak averaging changes which weights you keep.

Analogy

Think of hiking down a mountain in fog. Part A is about better footwork: how far and in which direction to step. Part B is about better trip planning: a better map, a better trailhead, an easier route first.

Part A · Setup

What are we minimizing? The empirical risk J(θ)

Training minimizes the average loss over the training set, J(θ). Every optimizer is just a strategy for walking downhill on this landscape.

In plain words

We would like the network to do well on all possible data, but we only have a training set. So we measure the error on every training example and average it. That average, J(θ), is the number training tries to push down.

Think of J as a landscape. Each setting of the weights θ is a location, and the height there is the average error. Every optimizer in this lecture is just a strategy for walking downhill on this landscape.

\[ J(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},y\sim\hat p_{\text{data}}}\big[L(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\theta),y)\big]=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}L\big(f(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\boldsymbol\theta),\,y^{(i)}\big) \](8.25)
Decode the symbols
\(\boldsymbol\theta\)
all the weights and biases of the network, stacked into one long vector
\(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\theta)\)
the network's prediction for input \(\mathbf x\)
\(L(\cdot, y)\)
the loss for one example: how wrong the prediction is compared with the true label \(y\)
\(m\)
number of training examples
\(\hat p_{\text{data}}\)
“the training set seen as a probability distribution”: every example equally likely

Where it fits in training

This is the loss step of the loop. In practice we estimate it on a minibatch instead of all m examples. The lecture keeps this plain version so the formulas stay simple. Everything also works if you add regularization terms such as weight decay.

Part A · Key idea

First-order vs. second-order information

The gradient tells you which way is downhill; the Hessian (curvature) tells you how far to go.

In plain words

The gradient \(\nabla J\) is the slope. It tells you which way is downhill and how steep it is. It does not tell you how far to go, so gradient descent needs a hand-picked learning rate ε.

The Hessian \(\boldsymbol H\) is the curvature: how fast the slope itself changes. A big curvature means a narrow valley, so take small steps. A small curvature means a wide valley, so take big steps. Second-order methods use H to choose the step size automatically.

\[ J(\theta)\approx \underbrace{J(\theta_0)}_{\text{height}}+\underbrace{J'(\theta_0)(\theta-\theta_0)}_{\text{slope: a line}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12 J''(\theta_0)(\theta-\theta_0)^2}_{\text{curvature: a parabola}} \]

This is a Taylor series written for one weight. With many weights, J′ becomes the gradient vector and J″ becomes the Hessian matrix \(H_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2 J}{\partial\theta_i\partial\theta_j}\), as shown on the next slide.

What do the eigenvalues of H mean?
all > 0
bowl-shaped in every direction: a local minimum nearby (“positive definite”)
all < 0
hill-shaped in every direction: a local maximum
mixed signs
up in some directions, down in others: a saddle point
big ratio
largest/smallest eigenvalue = condition number: a long narrow valley, which slows gradient descent

Analogy

Hiking downhill blindfolded. With only the slope, you feel the tilt under your foot and guess a stride length. If you also feel the curvature (how the tilt changes as you shuffle forward), you can estimate where the valley floor is and jump there directly.

Part A · 8.6.1

Newton's method

Approximate the loss by a bowl and jump to its bottom: θ* = θ₀ − H⁻¹∇J. One step on a perfect bowl.

In plain words

Around where you stand, replace the real landscape by a bowl (the second-order Taylor approximation). Then jump straight to the bottom of that bowl.

  • If the real loss is a bowl (quadratic, with positive-definite H), you arrive in one step.
  • If it is convex but not exactly quadratic, the bowl is only approximate, so you repeat. That repetition is the Newton training algorithm.
\[ J(\boldsymbol\theta)\approx J(\boldsymbol\theta_0)+(\boldsymbol\theta-\boldsymbol\theta_0)^\top\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_0)+\tfrac12(\boldsymbol\theta-\boldsymbol\theta_0)^\top\boldsymbol H(\boldsymbol\theta-\boldsymbol\theta_0) \](8.26)

Set the gradient of this bowl to zero: \(\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta_0)+\boldsymbol H(\boldsymbol\theta-\boldsymbol\theta_0)=0\). Solving gives the Newton update:

\[ \boldsymbol\theta^{*}=\boldsymbol\theta_0-\boldsymbol H^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_0) \](8.27)

Why multiplying by H⁻¹ is clever

H⁻¹ rescales the gradient separately in every direction. Steep, highly curved directions get shrunk, and flat directions get stretched. It works like an automatic, per-direction learning rate, so there is no ε to tune.

The algorithm (two steps, repeated)

  1. Compute the gradient \(\boldsymbol g\) on a minibatch.
  2. Compute the Hessian \(\boldsymbol H\) and its inverse \(\boldsymbol H^{-1}\).
  3. Update: \(\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta-\boldsymbol H^{-1}\boldsymbol g\). Go back to 1.

This only works as long as H stays positive definite. The next slide shows what happens when it does not.

Part A · 8.6.1

When Newton goes wrong: saddle points & regularization

Newton looks for any point with zero slope, so near a saddle it can walk the wrong way. Adding αI to H fixes the direction.

The problem

Newton only looks for the point where the slope of its local model is zero. It cannot tell a valley floor from a hilltop or a saddle. Near a saddle point, some eigenvalues of H are negative, and Newton happily jumps toward the saddle. That is the wrong direction.

This matters a lot in deep learning: high-dimensional loss surfaces are full of saddle points.

The fix: regularize the Hessian

Add a constant α along the diagonal. This raises every eigenvalue by α, so if α is big enough they all become positive and the step points downhill again. Approximations of Newton's method such as the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm use this idea.

\[ \boldsymbol\theta^{*}=\boldsymbol\theta_0-\big[\boldsymbol H+\alpha\boldsymbol I\big]^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_0) \](8.28)

The trade-off

If the negative curvature is strong, α must be large. But then \(\boldsymbol H+\alpha\boldsymbol I\approx\alpha\boldsymbol I\), so the update becomes \(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\nabla J\): plain gradient descent with learning rate 1/α. With very strong negative curvature, the step can even be smaller than a well-tuned gradient descent step. All of Newton's advantage is gone.

  • Builds onSaddle points and plateaus (L7): in high dimensions saddle points vastly outnumber local minima.
  • Builds onCritical points (L3): minima, maxima and saddles all have zero gradient.
Part A · 8.6.1

Why exact Newton doesn't scale to neural networks

A network with k weights has a k × k Hessian: impossible to store or invert when k is in the millions.

In plain words

With k parameters, the Hessian is a k × k table: one entry for every pair of weights. Even small networks have millions of parameters.

  • Memory: k² numbers.
  • Compute: inverting a k × k matrix costs about O(k³) operations.
  • Every step: θ changes after each update, so H must be recomputed and re-inverted every iteration.

Conclusion: only networks with a very small number of parameters can practically be trained with Newton's method.

So what's the rest of Part A?

Clever ways to get some of Newton's benefit without paying k² / k³:

  • Conjugate gradients never forms or inverts H at all.
  • BFGS builds a cheap running estimate of H⁻¹ (still k² memory).
  • L-BFGS builds that estimate from a few vectors (≈ k memory).
Part A · 8.6.2 (motivation)

Steepest descent with line search, and the zig-zag problem

Exact line search along the gradient makes every new direction perpendicular to the last one → zig-zag.

In plain words

A natural upgrade to fixed-learning-rate gradient descent: point in the downhill direction, then search along that line for the lowest point (try several ε and keep the best). This is called a line search.

It sounds optimal, but it zig-zags. At the lowest point along a line, the slope along that line is exactly zero, so the new gradient is perpendicular to the line you just searched. Each new direction is at 90° to the previous one, and part of each step undoes progress made by the step before.

\[ \epsilon^*=\arg\min_{\epsilon}\,J\big(\boldsymbol\theta-\epsilon\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta)\big) \]
\[ \text{at the end of the line search: }\ \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol d_{t-1}=0\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol d_t\perp\boldsymbol d_{t-1} \]

Analogy

Walking down a narrow canyon by always going straight downhill: you hit one wall, turn 90°, hit the other wall, turn 90° again… You bounce between the walls and only creep along the canyon floor.

Part A · 8.6.2

Conjugate gradients: directions that don't undo each other

Mix a bit of the previous direction into the new one so you never undo earlier progress (conjugate directions).

In plain words

Instead of following the pure gradient, mix in a bit of the previous direction. Choose the mix so the new direction does not spoil the minimization already done along the old one. Such directions are called conjugate.

Payoff: on a quadratic with k parameters, CG reaches the exact minimum in at most k line searches. In the 2-D demo that is just 2 steps. It never computes H⁻¹.

\[ \boldsymbol d_t=\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta)+\beta_t\,\boldsymbol d_{t-1} \](8.29) \[ \text{conjugate:}\quad \boldsymbol d_t^{\top}\boldsymbol H\,\boldsymbol d_{t-1}=0 \](8.30)

βt controls how much of the old direction is added back. The book's algorithm box moves along \(-\boldsymbol g_t+\beta_t\boldsymbol\rho_{t-1}\), which is the same idea written as a downhill direction.

Finding conjugate directions through the eigenvectors of H would be as expensive as Newton. Fortunately β can be computed from gradients only:

\[ \text{Fletcher–Reeves:}\quad \beta_t=\frac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_t)^{\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_t)}{\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_{t-1})^{\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_{t-1})} \](8.31) \[ \text{Polak–Ribière:}\quad \beta_t=\frac{\big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_t)-\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_{t-1})\big)^{\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_t)}{\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_{t-1})^{\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta_{t-1})} \](8.32)

Analogy for “conjugate”

Imagine squashing the stretched valley until it becomes a perfectly round bowl. In a round bowl, perpendicular moves never interfere with each other. Conjugate directions are just “perpendicular in that squashed space”, and H is what does the squashing.

Connections to other lectures

  • CompareMomentum (L8) also adds a fraction of the previous step: d = −g + β·dprev. CG chooses β cleverly and uses a line search; momentum uses a fixed α.
Part A · 8.6.2

Nonlinear conjugate gradients

On real (non-quadratic) losses CG needs resets, a warm start with SGD, and works in minibatch form.

In plain words

Real neural-network losses are not quadratic. Then the conjugate directions no longer guarantee that we stay at the minimum along the previous directions. The “at most k steps” promise is gone. Practical fixes:

  1. Occasional resets: restart CG with a line search along the plain gradient, which throws away stale direction memory.
  2. Warm start: run a few iterations of SGD first, then switch to nonlinear CG.
  3. Minibatches: CG was traditionally a full-batch method, but minibatch versions have been used successfully for training neural nets.

Fletcher–Reeves vs. Polak–Ribière

On a quadratic with exact line searches the two formulas agree. On curvy losses they differ. Polak–Ribière tends to behave better: when the gradient barely changes between steps, its β drops toward 0, which acts like an automatic reset.

Where it fits in training

CG replaces the update rule. It needs only gradients (O(k) memory), but every step needs a line search, which means several extra loss evaluations.

Part A · 8.6.3

BFGS: Newton's power without computing H⁻¹

Keep a running estimate M ≈ H⁻¹, improved after every step from how the gradient changed. Newton-like steps without inverting H.

In plain words

The expensive part of Newton is \(\boldsymbol H^{-1}\). Quasi-Newton methods, of which BFGS (Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno) is the most famous, keep a running estimate \(\boldsymbol M_t\approx\boldsymbol H^{-1}\) and refine it after every step with a cheap low-rank update.

How does it learn curvature without second derivatives? If moving by \(\boldsymbol s\) changed the gradient by \(\boldsymbol y\), then the curvature along \(\boldsymbol s\) is roughly “change in slope ÷ distance moved”. BFGS forces M to agree with this observation: \(\boldsymbol M_{t+1}\boldsymbol y=\boldsymbol s\).

\[ \boldsymbol\rho_t=\boldsymbol M_t\,\boldsymbol g_t,\qquad \boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_t+\epsilon^{*}\boldsymbol\rho_t \](8.33)

A line search picks the step size ε* along this direction, and the sign is chosen so the step goes downhill (equivalently, move along \(-\boldsymbol M_t\boldsymbol g_t\)).

For the curious: the BFGS update of M
\[ \boldsymbol M_{t+1}=\big(\boldsymbol I-r\,\boldsymbol s\boldsymbol y^{\top}\big)\boldsymbol M_t\big(\boldsymbol I-r\,\boldsymbol y\boldsymbol s^{\top}\big)+r\,\boldsymbol s\boldsymbol s^{\top},\qquad r=\frac{1}{\boldsymbol y^{\top}\boldsymbol s} \]

with \(\boldsymbol s=\boldsymbol\theta_{t+1}-\boldsymbol\theta_t\) and \(\boldsymbol y=\boldsymbol g_{t+1}-\boldsymbol g_t\). This is only a rank-2 correction, so it is cheap compared with inverting H, and M stays positive definite.

Strength

Like CG it uses line searches, but it does not need them to be very precise. It can spend less time refining each line search.

Weakness

It must store M: k × k numbers, O(k²) memory. That is impossible for networks with millions of parameters.

Connections to other lectures

  • CompareAdaGrad / RMSProp / Adam (L8): they also learn a per-direction scale from gradient history, but only a diagonal one — O(k) memory instead of O(k²).
Part A · 8.6.3

L-BFGS: limited-memory BFGS

Don’t store M at all: rebuild it from the last m (s, y) pairs. Memory O(mk).

In plain words

Don't store M at all. Each step, pretend the previous M was the identity and rebuild the effect of M from only the last few (s, y) pairs. Each pair is just two vectors of length k.

  • Memory: O(mk) with m pairs, instead of O(k²). With m = 1 this is the simplest version the lecture describes.
  • With exact line searches, the directions L-BFGS produces are mutually conjugate, just like CG.
  • Unlike CG, it stays well behaved when the line search only finds the minimum approximately.
  • Storing more pairs (bigger m) gives it more information about the Hessian.

Today

L-BFGS is still popular for smaller, full-batch, smooth problems, for example classic machine-learning models and some scientific and style-transfer problems. In big minibatch deep learning, noisy gradients spoil its curvature estimates, so SGD-style methods dominate.

Part B · 8.7

Optimization strategies & meta-algorithms

These are not new optimizers. They are general templates that can be specialized into algorithms, or subroutines that slot into almost any training procedure. You can combine all of them with SGD, Adam or anything from Part A.

Part B tricks do not replace the optimizer — they make the problem easier for whatever optimizer you use.

Batch normalization

Changes the model so layers stop fighting each other when they are updated together.

Polyak averaging

Changes which weights you keep: the average of recent points instead of the last one.

Supervised pretraining

Changes the starting point: solve simpler models or tasks first, then build on them.

Designing models

Choose an architecture whose landscape is easy: ReLU, skip connections, auxiliary heads.

Continuation methods

Change the objective: solve a blurred, easy version first and sharpen it gradually.

Curriculum learning

Change the data order: easy examples first, hard ones gradually.

Part B · 8.7.1 Batch normalization (motivation)

Why deep networks are hard to update: layers interfere

Each layer’s gradient assumes the other layers stay put, but they all move at once — their effects multiply.

In plain words

The gradient for each weight answers: “how should this weight change, assuming every other layer stays fixed?” But we update all layers at the same time. In a deep network the layers multiply together, so their changes interact and create effects the gradient never predicted.

The toy example from the lecture

One unit per layer, no activation function. The output is linear in x but a product of the weights:

\[ \hat y=x\,w_1w_2w_3\cdots w_l \](8.34)

Update \(\boldsymbol w\leftarrow\boldsymbol w-\epsilon\boldsymbol g\). First-order theory says ŷ drops by \(\epsilon\,\boldsymbol g^{\top}\boldsymbol g\), so to drop it by 0.1 we would pick \(\epsilon=0.1/\boldsymbol g^{\top}\boldsymbol g\). What really happens:

\[ \hat y_{\text{new}}=x(w_1-\epsilon g_1)(w_2-\epsilon g_2)\cdots(w_l-\epsilon g_l) \](8.35)

Multiplying this out gives higher-order terms, up to order l, such as \(\epsilon^2 g_1g_2\prod_{i=3}^{l}w_i\). That term is tiny if the other weights are small, and exponentially large if they are bigger than 1.

Why that is a big deal

The effect of updating one layer depends strongly on all the other layers, so choosing a good learning rate becomes very hard. Second-order methods can account for 2nd-order interactions, but they are expensive and approximate, and deep networks also have 3rd, 4th, … order interactions. An n-th order method for n > 2 seems hopeless. We need a different idea: change the model instead of the optimizer.

  • Builds onChain rule (L5): the gradient of a product of layers is a product of factors — change several factors at once and the result is not what any single factor predicted.

Part B · 8.7.1

Batch normalization: the recipe

For each unit, subtract the minibatch mean and divide by the std, so its input always has mean 0, std 1.

In plain words

Take a layer's activations for a whole minibatch. For each unit (each column), subtract its average over the batch and divide by its standard deviation. Every unit then has mean 0 and std 1 over the batch, no matter what the earlier layers do.

That is a reparametrization: it greatly reduces the problem of coordinating updates across many layers. It can be applied to any input or hidden layer.

\[ \boldsymbol H'=\frac{\boldsymbol H-\boldsymbol\mu}{\boldsymbol\sigma} \](8.36) \[ \boldsymbol\mu=\frac1m\sum_i\boldsymbol H_{i,:}\qquad \boldsymbol\sigma=\sqrt{\delta+\frac1m\sum_i(\boldsymbol H-\boldsymbol\mu)_i^2} \](8.37–8.38)
Decode the symbols
\(\boldsymbol H\)
minibatch of activations as a matrix: one row per example, one column per unit
\(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\sigma\)
vectors holding each unit's mean and standard deviation over the batch. They are broadcast: the same μj, σj are used in every row for column j
\(\delta\)
a tiny number such as 10⁻⁸ that avoids the undefined gradient of √z at z = 0 and division by zero
\(\boldsymbol H'\)
the normalized activations; the rest of the network uses H′ exactly as it used H before

Where it fits in training

BN is part of the model, in the forward pass, and its statistics come from the current minibatch. The optimizer is unchanged. Because the layer below can no longer change this layer's input scale or offset, the cross-layer interference from the previous slide largely disappears.

Connections to other lectures

  • Builds onInitialization scale (L8): careful init keeps activations well-scaled at the start; BN keeps them that way during training.
  • Used inConvolutional networks (L10): BN is a standard layer in modern CNNs.
Part B · 8.7.1

The key trick: back-propagate through the normalization

Because we back-propagate through μ and σ, the gradient can never waste effort changing a unit’s mean or scale.

In plain words

μ and σ are computed from H, and we back-propagate through those computations too. They are not treated as constants.

Consequence: the gradient can never propose an update whose only effect is to raise a unit's mean or standard deviation. The normalization would cancel such a change, so backprop gives that component of the gradient a value of zero. The lecture calls this the major innovation of batch norm.

What people did before

  1. Add a penalty to the cost that encourages normalized statistics. The result is usually imperfect normalization.
  2. Renormalize after every gradient step. This wastes time: the optimizer keeps proposing changes to mean and variance, and the renormalization keeps undoing them.

BN sidesteps both: the units are standardized by definition, so there is nothing to fight.

Analogy

Keeping a room at 20 °C. A penalty is like asking people politely not to touch the heater, so the temperature still drifts. Renormalizing is like someone keeps turning it up while you keep turning it down: lots of wasted effort. BN removes the dial, so nobody wastes energy trying.

Connections to other lectures

  • Builds onComputational graphs (L5): μ and σ are just more nodes in the graph, so backprop handles them automatically.
Part B · 8.7.1

Batch norm at test time: running averages

At test time use running averages of μ and σ collected during training, not the test batch.

In plain words

During training, μ and σ come from the current minibatch. At test time we often want to predict one example at a time, and its output should not depend on what else happens to be in the batch.

So during training we keep running averages of μ and σ, and at test time we use those fixed values instead.

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\leftarrow 0.9\,\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}+0.1\,\boldsymbol\mu_{\text{batch}},\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\sigma}^2\leftarrow 0.9\,\hat{\boldsymbol\sigma}^2+0.1\,\boldsymbol\sigma^2_{\text{batch}} \]

0.1 is a typical “momentum” value (it is PyTorch's default). Any exponential moving average works.

Practical tip

In PyTorch, model.train() uses batch statistics and updates the running averages, while model.eval() switches to the running averages. Forgetting model.eval() before testing is a classic bug, and the demo shows why.

Part B · 8.7.1

Keeping the network expressive: γ and β, and where BN goes

Learned γ and β give back the freedom normalization took away, with much easier learning dynamics.

Back to the linear toy network

Normalize \(h_{l-1}\) and the output becomes simply \(\hat y=w_l\hat h_{l-1}\). Whatever the lower layers do, \(\hat h_{l-1}\) stays a unit Gaussian, so learning becomes very easy. The rare exceptions: setting a lower weight to 0 makes the output degenerate, and flipping a weight's sign flips the relationship.

The catch: in a linear network the lower layers can only change mean and variance, and those are now normalized away, so the lower layers become useless. In a real network with nonlinear activations, lower layers still perform useful nonlinear transformations, so they keep their value.

γ and β: give the freedom back

Forcing mean 0 and std 1 might limit what a layer can express. So we use

\[ \gamma\boldsymbol H'+\boldsymbol\beta \]

where γ and β are learned. “Wait, didn't we just remove the mean, only to let β set it again?” Yes, and that is the point. Both versions can represent the same functions, but the learning dynamics differ:

  • Old: the mean of H depends on a complicated interaction of all the layers below.
  • New: the mean of γH′ + β is decided by β alone, one parameter that is easy for gradient descent to control.

Where to put it

  • A layer computes \(\phi(\boldsymbol{XW}+\boldsymbol b)\). Normalize XW + b, not X. In fact, drop b and use \(\phi(\mathrm{BN}(\boldsymbol{XW}))\), because b is subtracted out by μ and β already does its job. (Try the “shift” slider on the recipe slide: Ĥ never moves.)
  • Convolutional nets: use the same μ and σ at every spatial location of a feature map, so the map's statistics do not depend on position.

Connections to other lectures

  • Used inCNNs (L10): per-channel normalization shared over all positions, the same idea as parameter sharing in convolution.
Part B · 8.7.3

Polyak averaging

Average the points the optimizer visits — the average sits closer to the bottom than any single bouncing iterate.

In plain words

Instead of keeping only the last point the optimizer visited, keep the average of the points it visited. If the optimizer keeps leaping back and forth across a valley without ever landing on the bottom, the average of its positions on both sides sits close to the bottom.

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}^{(t)}=\frac1t\sum_i\boldsymbol\theta^{(i)} \]

On convex problems, for example gradient descent on a convex loss, this has strong convergence guarantees.

Non-convex problems

The path can wander through very different regions, and points from the distant past may be separated from the current one by big barriers in the loss, so averaging them in is not useful. So use an exponentially decaying running average, which gradually forgets the past:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}^{(t)}=\alpha\,\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}^{(t-1)}+(1-\alpha)\,\boldsymbol\theta^{(t)} \](8.39)

Today

Keeping an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights, and evaluating with the averaged copy, is a widely used trick in modern training, for example in image-generation models. It costs one extra copy of θ. The optimizer itself does not change.

Part B · 8.7.4

Supervised pretraining: solve easier problems first

If the full problem is too hard, solve simpler problems first and use their answers as the starting point.

In plain words

Sometimes training the full model directly on the full task is too ambitious. It can be better to

  • train a simpler model first and then make it more complex, or
  • train on a simpler task first and then move to the real one.

All such “simple first” strategies are called pretraining.

Greedy algorithms

A greedy algorithm splits a problem into parts and solves each part on its own. Combining the individually-best parts is not guaranteed to be the best overall, but it is much cheaper and usually good enough.

Then a fine-tuning stage optimizes everything jointly. Starting the joint optimization from the greedy solution can greatly speed it up and improve the result.

Where it fits in training

Pretraining is a smarter initialization. It puts θ in a region where ordinary local descent can find a good solution. Why should it help? The hypothesis is that it gives better guidance to the intermediate layers of a deep hierarchy.

Part B · 8.7.4

Variations: deeper init, transfer learning, teacher → student hints

Copy layers from a trained network (transfer) or give a student network hints from a teacher.

1 · Initialize a deeper network from a shallower one

Pretrain a whole deep conv net with 11 weight layers. Copy its first 4 and last 3 layers into a much deeper net with up to 19 weight layers. Initialize the middle randomly, then train everything jointly. Another option: give each new stage both the raw input and the outputs of the previously trained stages.

2 · Transfer learning

Pretrain an 8-layer conv net on one set of tasks (part of the 1,000 ImageNet classes). Build a same-size network for different classes, copy the first k layers, and initialize the upper layers randomly. Then train it jointly on the new task, which has fewer examples.

3 · Teacher → student (hints)

Train a wide, shallow teacher, which is easy to optimize. Then train a thin, deep student (11–19 layers) to predict the task output and, through extra regressor parameters, the teacher's middle layer. These hints tell the student's hidden layers how to be used.

\[ \text{conceptually:}\quad \mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\text{task}}(\hat y,y)+\lambda\,\big\|\,\boldsymbol h^{\text{teacher}}_{\text{mid}}-r\big(\boldsymbol h^{\text{student}}_{\text{mid}}\big)\big\|^2 \]

A thin, deep network looks harder to train, but it may generalize better and costs far less to run if it has few parameters.

Today

These ideas are everywhere: starting from a pretrained backbone and fine-tuning it (transfer learning), and training small students from big teachers (knowledge distillation).

Part B · 8.7.5

Designing models to aid optimization

It is usually more effective to change the model than the optimizer: linear-ish units, skip connections, auxiliary heads.

The big claim

In practice it is more important to choose a model family that is easy to optimize than to use a powerful optimizer. Most of the progress of the last 30 years came from changing the model, not the optimizer. SGD with momentum, already used in the 1980s, is still used in state-of-the-art systems.

Design choices that help

  • Mostly linear pieces: linear transformations between layers, and activations that are differentiable almost everywhere with a significant slope over large regions. ReLU, maxout and the LSTM all move toward linearity, unlike sigmoid networks. Gradients then flow through many layers, as long as the Jacobians have reasonable singular values.
  • Local information points to far solutions: a linear function keeps increasing in one direction. Even when the output is very wrong, the gradient clearly says which way to move. Modern nets are designed so that local gradients point toward distant solutions.
  • Skip connections: shorten the path from low layers to the output, which reduces vanishing gradients.
  • Auxiliary heads (GoogLeNet, deeply-supervised nets): extra copies of the output attached to middle layers and trained on the same task, so lower layers get a large gradient. They are thrown away after training.

Part B · 8.7.6

Continuation methods: start with an easy version of the problem

Solve a blurred, easier version of the loss first, then gradually sharpen it.

In plain words

Some difficulties come from the global shape of the loss, and no clever local step can fix them. The main strategy is to start somewhere connected to the solution by a short path that local descent can find.

Continuation builds a series of loss functions over the same parameters, from easy to hard. Each problem is solved starting from the previous answer:

\[ J^{(0)},\,J^{(1)},\,\dots,\,J^{(n)}=J \]

“Easier” means well behaved over more of θ-space. The answer to each problem is a good starting point for the next.

The classic recipe: blur it

Make an easier J by averaging it over nearby points, a Gaussian blur, approximated by sampling:

\[ J^{(i)}(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol\theta'\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\theta';\,\boldsymbol\theta,\,\sigma^{(i)2})}\,J(\boldsymbol\theta') \](8.40)

Many bumpy non-convex functions become approximately convex when blurred, yet the blur keeps enough information about where the global minimum is. Then reduce σ step by step.

Analogy

Squint at a mountain range: the little bumps disappear and you see the big valley. Walk toward it, then slowly open your eyes and fine-tune your position among the details.

Part B · 8.7.6

When continuation fails, and why it still helps

Blurring can fail (too costly, never convex, wrong basin), but it still helps by smoothing flat regions and noise.

Three ways it can break

  1. Works, but too expensive. The blurred sequence may track the global minimum perfectly but need so many stages that the total cost stays high. NP-hard problems remain NP-hard.
  2. Never becomes convex. Some functions don't get nicer no matter how much you blur. Example: \(J(\theta)=-\theta^2\).
  3. Convex, but tracks to the wrong place. After blurring, the minimum may lead to a local rather than the global minimum of the original function.

Cases 2 and 3 mean the method is simply not applicable to that problem.

Why it is still useful

Continuation was invented to beat local minima, and local minima are no longer believed to be the main problem in neural-network training. Easier objectives still help, because they can

  • eliminate flat regions,
  • reduce the variance of gradient estimates,
  • improve the conditioning of the Hessian,
  • make local update directions point more toward the global solution.
Part B · 8.7.6

Curriculum learning (shaping)

Show easy examples first, and gradually mix in harder ones — a stochastic mix works best.

In plain words

Plan learning like a school course: start with simple concepts, then move to more complex ones that build on them. The lecture notes this matches how humans teach.

Why it is a continuation method

The early objectives \(J^{(i)}\) are made easier by increasing the influence of simple examples, either by giving their losses larger weights or by sampling them more often. The final objective is the ordinary loss over the full training set.

The surprising result

In the experiment discussed in the lecture, where harder examples have longer-term dependencies, a deterministic curriculum (easy first, then hard) gave no improvement over ordinary training. A stochastic curriculum did much better: always a random mix of easy and hard examples, with the average share of hard ones gradually increased.

Analogy

A language course that only drills the alphabet for a month, then only grammar, then only essays, makes you forget earlier skills and struggle at each jump. A course that mixes a little of everything, and shifts toward harder material week by week, works better.

Curvature makes each step smarter, but exact curvature is too expensive and can be misleading at saddles, so we approximate it (CG, BFGS, L-BFGS) or skip it.
