Numerical Optimization & SGD

Lectures 1–2 told us what to minimize (a loss such as MSE or a negative log-likelihood). This lecture is about how: follow the slope downhill (gradient descent), understand where it can stop (critical points), how curvature (the Hessian) makes it fast or slow, how to respect constraints, and how to scale to huge datasets with stochastic gradient descent.

Where this lecture fits in training

This lecture is the engine of the loop: steps 6–7 compute the gradient and move the weights. SGD also explains step 3 — why we use small random minibatches.

First-order methods

Use only the gradient (slope). Gradient descent and SGD — the methods actually used to train deep networks.

Second-order methods

Also use the Hessian (curvature). Newton’s method — fast near minima, but expensive and attracted to saddles (Lecture 9 goes deeper).

Why it matters for deep learning

Deep-learning losses have many local minima and saddle points, and few guarantees. We settle for a value that is “very low”, not provably minimal.

4.3 · Gradient-based optimization

The derivative tells you which way is down

The derivative \(f'(x)\) is the slope: a small move ε changes f by about \(\epsilon f'(x)\). So step against the slope to go down. That is gradient descent.

In plain words

Optimization = minimizing (or maximizing) a function \(f(x)\) by changing x. To maximize, minimize \(-f(x)\). The function is called the objective function or criterion; when minimizing, also the cost, loss or error function. The best x is written \(x^*=\arg\min f(x)\).

Because \(f(x-\epsilon\,\text{sign}(f'(x)))<f(x)\) for small enough ε, repeatedly taking small steps opposite to the derivative decreases f.

\[ f(x+\epsilon)\approx f(x)+\epsilon f'(x) \]slope = how a small input change scales into an output change \[ x\leftarrow x-\epsilon f'(x) \]gradient descent in 1-D

Objective / cost / loss

Calculation, step by step

  1. \(f(x)=x^2\), start x = 3, ε = 0.1. Slope \(f'(3)=2\cdot3=6\).
  2. Update: \(x\leftarrow3-0.1\times6=2.4\). f drops from 9 to \(2.4^2=5.76\).
  3. First-order prediction: \(f(x)-\epsilon f'(x)^2=9-0.1\times36=5.4\). The real value 5.76 is a little higher because the parabola curves up — curvature matters (Hessian slide).
  4. Next step: \(f'(2.4)=4.8\Rightarrow x=2.4-0.48=1.92\) … each step multiplies x by \(1-2\epsilon=0.8\).

4.3 · Critical points

Where the slope is zero: minima, maxima, saddles

Where \(f'=0\) the derivative gives no direction. Such critical points can be minima, maxima or saddle points; the second derivative (in many dimensions, the Hessian’s eigenvalues) tells which.

In plain words

  • Local minimum: lower than all neighbours — tiny steps can’t decrease f. Local maximum: higher than all neighbours. Saddle point: neither.
  • Global minimum: the absolute lowest value (there may be several).
  • Deep-learning losses have many non-optimal local minima and many saddle points surrounded by flat regions, so we usually settle for “very low”, not “minimal”.
  • Second-derivative test (1-D): \(f'=0,f''>0\) → min; \(f''<0\) → max; \(f''=0\) → inconclusive.
  • In many dimensions: Hessian positive definite (all eigenvalues > 0) → min; negative definite → max; mixed signs → saddle; any zero eigenvalue with the rest same sign → inconclusive.

Terms on this slide

Eigenvalue
Curvature along one special direction (eigenvector) of the Hessian.

Calculation, step by step

  1. \(f(x)=x^3-3x\): \(f'(x)=3x^2-3=0\Rightarrow x=\pm1\).
  2. \(f''(x)=6x\): at x = 1, \(f''=6>0\) → local minimum (f = −2); at x = −1, \(f''=-6<0\) → local maximum (f = 2).
  3. 2-D: \(f=x^2-y^2\) at (0, 0): gradient (2x, −2y) = 0; Hessian \(\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&-2\end{bmatrix}\), eigenvalues 2 and −2 → saddle.

4.3 · Many inputs

The gradient and steepest descent

The gradient \(\nabla_{\mathbf x}f\) collects all partial derivatives. It points straight uphill; the negative gradient is the direction of fastest decrease. Step: \(\mathbf x'=\mathbf x-\epsilon\nabla_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)\).

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}f(\mathbf x+\alpha\mathbf u)\Big|_{\alpha=0}=\mathbf u^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x) \]directional derivative \[ \min_{\mathbf u,\ \mathbf u^\top\mathbf u=1}\mathbf u^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)=\min_{\mathbf u}\|\mathbf u\|_2\,\|\nabla_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)\|_2\cos\theta \](4.3–4.4) \[ \mathbf x'=\mathbf x-\epsilon\nabla_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x) \](4.5) steepest descent

In plain words

  • Partial derivative \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}f\): how f changes if only \(x_i\) increases.
  • Critical points in many dimensions: every element of the gradient is 0.
  • With ‖u‖ = 1, only cos θ depends on u, and it’s smallest (−1) when u points opposite to the gradient.
  • Choosing ε: a small constant; or solve for the ε that makes the directional derivative vanish; or try several and keep the best — a line search.
  • Converges when the gradient is (almost) zero. Sometimes we can skip iterating and solve \(\nabla f=0\) directly (like the normal equations).
  • The discrete-space version (repeatedly make the best small move) is called hill climbing.

Terms on this slide

Gradient \(\nabla_{\mathbf x}f\)
Vector of partial derivatives \((\partial f/\partial x_1,\dots,\partial f/\partial x_n)\).
Unit vector u
A direction with length 1.
Directional derivative
Slope of f when moving along u.
Line search
Try several step sizes along a direction, keep the best.

Calculation, step by step

  1. \(f(x,y)=x^2+3y^2\) at (1, 1): \(\nabla f=(2x,6y)=(2,6)\), \(\|\nabla f\|=\sqrt{4+36}=6.32\).
  2. Along u = (1, 0): slope \(=\mathbf u^\top\nabla f=2\) (uphill). Along \(-\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|=(-0.316,-0.949)\): slope \(=-6.32\) — the steepest possible.
  3. Step with ε = 0.1: \((1,1)-0.1(2,6)=(0.8,0.4)\); f falls from 4 to \(0.64+0.48=1.12\).

4.3.1 · Beyond the gradient

Jacobian and Hessian: derivatives of derivatives

The Hessian (all second derivatives) measures curvature. It predicts how good a gradient step will be: with too much curvature in the step direction, a step can even go uphill.

\[ J_{i,j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}f(\mathbf x)_i\qquad\boldsymbol H(f)(\mathbf x)_{i,j}=\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}f(\mathbf x)=\boldsymbol H_{j,i} \](4.6–4.7) \[ f(\mathbf x)\approx f(\mathbf x^{(0)})+(\mathbf x-\mathbf x^{(0)})^{\top}\mathbf g+\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\mathbf x^{(0)})^{\top}\boldsymbol H(\mathbf x-\mathbf x^{(0)}) \](4.8) \[ f(\mathbf x^{(0)}-\epsilon\mathbf g)\approx f(\mathbf x^{(0)})-\epsilon\,\mathbf g^{\top}\mathbf g+\tfrac12\epsilon^2\,\mathbf g^{\top}\boldsymbol H\mathbf g,\qquad\epsilon^*=\frac{\mathbf g^{\top}\mathbf g}{\mathbf g^{\top}\boldsymbol H\mathbf g} \](4.9–4.10)

In plain words

  • Jacobian: all first derivatives of a function whose input and output are vectors (\(\mathbb R^m\to\mathbb R^n\) gives an n×m matrix).
  • 1-D curvature: f″ = 0 → a straight line, a step of ε along a gradient of 1 decreases f by exactly ε. f″ < 0 → it decreases more; f″ > 0 → less.
  • The Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient; symmetric almost everywhere in deep learning → real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors. The second derivative along unit d is \(\mathbf d^\top\boldsymbol H\mathbf d\): an eigenvalue along an eigenvector, a weighted average of eigenvalues otherwise.
  • (4.9) has three parts: the current value, the improvement from the slope, and a curvature correction. If the correction is too big the step goes uphill; if \(\mathbf g^\top\boldsymbol H\mathbf g\le0\) the model says “bigger is always better” (not trustworthy for large ε).
  • Worst case (g along the top eigenvector): \(\epsilon^*=1/\lambda_{\max}\). The Hessian’s eigenvalues set the scale of the learning rate.

Terms on this slide

Jacobian J
Matrix of first partial derivatives of a vector function.
Hessian H
Matrix of second partial derivatives of a scalar function.
Curvature
How fast the slope changes.
Taylor approximation
Approximate f near a point using its value, slope and curvature.
g
Gradient at \(\mathbf x^{(0)}\).

Calculation, step by step

  1. Jacobian of \(\mathbf f(x,y)=(x^2y,\;5x+\sin y)\): \(\begin{bmatrix}2xy&x^2\\5&\cos y\end{bmatrix}\); at (1, 0): \(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\5&1\end{bmatrix}\).
  2. Hessian of \(f=x^2+3xy+2y^2\): \(\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\3&4\end{bmatrix}\) (symmetric).
  3. Optimal step: \(\boldsymbol H=\text{diag}(1,10)\), \(\mathbf x^{(0)}=(1,1)\) → \(\mathbf g=\boldsymbol H\mathbf x=(1,10)\), \(\mathbf g^\top\mathbf g=101\), \(\mathbf g^\top\boldsymbol H\mathbf g=1\cdot1+10\cdot100=1001\) → \(\epsilon^*=101/1001=0.101\approx1/\lambda_{\max}=0.1\).

4.3.1 · Ill-conditioning

Condition number, Newton’s method, Lipschitz

If curvature is very different in different directions (large condition number), gradient descent zig-zags with a tiny step. Newton’s method rescales by \(\boldsymbol H^{-1}\) and jumps to the bottom of the local bowl.

\[ \kappa=\frac{\max_i|\lambda_i|}{\min_i|\lambda_i|} \]condition number \[ \mathbf x^*=\mathbf x^{(0)}-\boldsymbol H(f)(\mathbf x^{(0)})^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x^{(0)}) \](4.12) Newton \[ \forall\mathbf x,\mathbf y:\ |f(\mathbf x)-f(\mathbf y)|\le\mathcal L\,\|\mathbf x-\mathbf y\|_2 \](4.13) Lipschitz continuity

In plain words

  • With a poor (large) condition number, the derivative grows fast in one direction and slowly in another; gradient descent doesn’t know it should prefer the slow direction.
  • The step must be small enough not to overshoot in the steep direction, which makes progress in the flat direction tiny.
  • Newton’s method: positive-definite quadratic → reaches the minimum in one step; otherwise iterate. Great near a minimum, harmful near a saddle (it’s attracted to it). Gradient descent is not attracted to saddles unless the gradient points at them.
  • First-order algorithms use only the gradient; second-order also use the Hessian.
  • Deep learning has few guarantees; sometimes we get some by assuming Lipschitz functions (bounded rate of change) — a weak, easy-to-satisfy constraint.

Terms on this slide

Condition number κ
Ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue magnitude.
Ill-conditioned
κ is large: a long, narrow valley.
Newton’s method
Jump to the minimum of the local quadratic model.
Lipschitz constant \(\mathcal L\)
Maximum rate at which f can change per unit of distance.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Hessian eigenvalues 1 and 100 → \(\kappa=100\).
  2. Gradient descent must use \(\epsilon<2/100=0.02\) or it diverges along the steep direction.
  3. Along the flat direction each step multiplies the error by \(1-0.02\times1=0.98\): reducing it 100× needs \(\ln0.01/\ln0.98\approx228\) steps. Newton: 1 step.
  4. \(f(x)=\sin x\) is Lipschitz with \(\mathcal L=1\), because \(|\cos x|\le1\).

4.4 · Constrained optimization

Optimizing inside a feasible set: projection and KKT

To minimize only over allowed points (e.g. ‖x‖ ≤ 1): project after each step, reparametrize, or use the generalized Lagrangian (KKT), which turns a constrained problem into an unconstrained min–max problem.

\[ \mathbb S=\{\mathbf x\mid\forall i,\ g^{(i)}(\mathbf x)=0\ \text{and}\ \forall j,\ h^{(j)}(\mathbf x)\le0\} \] \[ L(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\lambda,\boldsymbol\alpha)=f(\mathbf x)+\sum_i\lambda_i g^{(i)}(\mathbf x)+\sum_j\alpha_j h^{(j)}(\mathbf x) \](4.14) generalized Lagrangian \[ \min_{\mathbf x}\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda}\max_{\boldsymbol\alpha,\boldsymbol\alpha\ge0}L(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\lambda,\boldsymbol\alpha)=\min_{\mathbf x\in\mathbb S}f(\mathbf x) \](4.15–4.16) \[ \max_{\boldsymbol\lambda}\max_{\boldsymbol\alpha\ge0}L=\begin{cases}f(\mathbf x)&\text{if all constraints hold}\\ \infty&\text{if any is violated}\end{cases}\qquad\boldsymbol\alpha\odot\boldsymbol h(\mathbf x)=\mathbf 0 \](4.17–4.18), complementary slackness

In plain words

  • Points inside the allowed set \(\mathbb S\) are feasible. A common constraint is a norm limit such as \(\|\mathbf x\|\le1\).
  • Projection: take a normal gradient step, then project back into \(\mathbb S\). (With a line search, only try feasible steps, or project every point on the line.)
  • Reparametrize: e.g. for unit-norm \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^2\), minimize \(g(\theta)=f([\cos\theta,\sin\theta])\) over θ. Needs creativity for each problem.
  • KKT: new variables λ (equality) and α ≥ 0 (inequality) — the KKT multipliers. The inner max punishes any violation with ∞, so infeasible points can’t win.
  • Active inequality: \(h(\mathbf x)=0\) at the solution. An inactive one has \(\alpha=0\). Either the solution sits on the boundary (and α pushes back), or the constraint has no influence.
  • KKT conditions (necessary, not always sufficient): ∇L = 0; all constraints on x and multipliers hold; complementary slackness \(\boldsymbol\alpha\odot\boldsymbol h=\mathbf 0\).

Terms on this slide

Feasible point
A point that satisfies all constraints.
Equality / inequality constraint
\(g(\mathbf x)=0\) / \(h(\mathbf x)\le0\).
KKT multipliers λ, α
Extra variables that price each constraint.
⊙
Element-wise (Hadamard) product.
Active constraint
One that holds with equality at the solution.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Minimize \(f(x)=x\) subject to \(x\ge1\), i.e. \(h(x)=1-x\le0\).
  2. \(L=x+\alpha(1-x)\). \(\partial L/\partial x=1-\alpha=0\Rightarrow\alpha=1\ge0\).
  3. Since α > 0, slackness \(\alpha\,h(x)=0\) forces \(h(x)=0\Rightarrow x=1\): the constraint is active, the answer sits on the boundary.
  4. Projection example: step lands at (1.2, 0.9), norm 1.5 > 1 → project to \((1.2,0.9)/1.5=(0.8,0.6)\).

4.5 · Example

Linear least squares: gradient descent, Newton and a constraint

One problem, three tools: gradient descent walks down \(\frac12\|\mathbf A\mathbf x-\mathbf b\|^2\); Newton solves it in one step; with \(\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x\le1\), the Lagrange multiplier λ acts exactly like a weight-decay coefficient.

\[ f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\|\mathbf A\mathbf x-\mathbf b\|_2^2,\qquad\nabla_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf A^{\top}(\mathbf A\mathbf x-\mathbf b)=\mathbf A^{\top}\mathbf A\mathbf x-\mathbf A^{\top}\mathbf b \](4.21–4.22) \[ \text{repeat: }\ \mathbf x\leftarrow\mathbf x-\epsilon\big(\mathbf A^{\top}\mathbf A\mathbf x-\mathbf A^{\top}\mathbf b\big)\ \text{ until }\|\nabla f\|<\delta \]Algorithm 4.1 \[ L(\mathbf x,\lambda)=f(\mathbf x)+\lambda(\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf x-1),\qquad\mathbf x=(\mathbf A^{\top}\mathbf A+2\lambda\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf A^{\top}\mathbf b,\qquad\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda}=\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf x-1 \](4.23–4.27)

In plain words

  • Specialized linear algebra solves this fast, but it’s a perfect test case for gradient methods.
  • f is exactly quadratic, so Newton’s quadratic model is exact → global minimum in one step.
  • With the constraint: first try the smallest-norm unconstrained solution \(\mathbf x=\mathbf A^+\mathbf b\) (Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse). If it’s feasible, done. Otherwise the constraint is active.
  • Setting \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf x=0\) gives \(\mathbf x(\lambda)\). If \(\|\mathbf x\|>1\), \(\partial L/\partial\lambda>0\) → increase λ (gradient ascent on λ) → bigger penalty → smaller x. Repeat until \(\|\mathbf x\|=1\).

Terms on this slide

\(\mathbf A^+\)
Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse: gives the smallest-norm least-squares solution.
δ
Tolerance: stop when the gradient is smaller than δ.
Gradient ascent on λ
Increase λ while the constraint is violated.

Calculation, step by step

  1. \(\mathbf A=\mathbf I\), \(\mathbf b=(3,4)\). Unconstrained answer \(\mathbf x=\mathbf b\), norm 5 > 1 → infeasible.
  2. \(\mathbf x(\lambda)=(\mathbf I+2\lambda\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf b=\frac{\mathbf b}{1+2\lambda}\), norm \(\frac{5}{1+2\lambda}\).
  3. Need norm 1: \(1+2\lambda=5\Rightarrow\lambda=2\), \(\mathbf x=(0.6,0.8)\). λ = 2 is exactly the weight-decay strength that enforces the constraint.
  4. One GD step from x = 0 with ε = 0.5: \(\mathbf x\leftarrow0-0.5(\mathbf x-\mathbf b)=(1.5,2)\).

Connections

  • Builds onNormal equations (L1): the same problem solved in closed form.
  • Leads toL2 regularization (L6): \((\mathbf A^\top\mathbf A+2\lambda\mathbf I)^{-1}\) is the weight-decay solution.
5.9 · Stochastic gradient descent

SGD: estimate the gradient from a small minibatch

The gradient is an average over all examples, so a small random minibatch gives a cheap, unbiased estimate. Cost per update depends on the minibatch size m′, not on the dataset size m.

\[ J(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbb E_{\mathbf x,y\sim\hat p_{\text{data}}}L(\mathbf x,y,\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}L\big(\mathbf x^{(i)},y^{(i)},\boldsymbol\theta\big),\quad L=-\log p(y\mid\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\theta) \](5.96) \[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}L\big(\mathbf x^{(i)},y^{(i)},\boldsymbol\theta\big)\quad\text{— costs }O(m) \](5.97) \[ \mathbf g=\frac{1}{m'}\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{m'}L\big(\mathbf x^{(i)},y^{(i)},\boldsymbol\theta\big),\qquad\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta-\epsilon\,\mathbf g \](5.98–5.99)

In plain words

  • Generalization needs big datasets, but exact gradients on big datasets are slow: billions of examples → one step takes forever.
  • Sample a minibatch \(\mathbb B=\{\mathbf x^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf x^{(m')}\}\) uniformly from the training set, with m′ from 1 to a few hundred, kept fixed as m grows.
  • Updates may never reach an exact local minimum in reasonable time, but they find a very low cost quickly.
  • As m → ∞, the model reaches its best test error before SGD has even seen every example → asymptotic cost of training is O(1) in m.
  • This is why deep learning scaled: from medium datasets (tens of thousands of examples, 2006) to huge industrial ones.

Terms on this slide

Per-example loss L
Loss on one example, here the negative log-likelihood.
Minibatch \(\mathbb B\), size m′
Small random subset used for one update.
Stochastic
Random: each update uses a different random minibatch.
O(m)
Cost grows proportionally with m.
Epoch
One pass over the whole training set (m/m′ updates).

Calculation, step by step

  1. m = 1 000 000 examples, m′ = 100.
  2. Exact gradient: 1 000 000 per-example gradients per step. Minibatch: 100 → 10 000× cheaper.
  3. Price: noise. The minibatch average has standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{100}=0.1\sigma\) (σ = std of per-example gradients) — usually good enough to point downhill.
  4. One epoch = \(10^6/100=10\,000\) updates for the cost of a single exact gradient step.

Wrap-up

Summary: how it all connects

Follow \(-\nabla f\) downhill; it can stop at any critical point. Curvature (Hessian eigenvalues, condition number) decides how fast and with what step size. Constraints become multipliers. Minibatches make every step cheap.
