Sequence Modeling: Recurrent and Recursive Nets

A recurrent neural network (RNN) reads a sequence one step at a time, keeping a state that summarizes what it has seen, and uses the same parameters at every step. That makes it work for any sequence length — and creates the famous problem of long-term dependencies, which gated units like the LSTM solve.

10 · Recurrent neural networks

Sharing parameters across time

An RNN processes a sequence \(\mathbf x^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf x^{(\tau)}\) with the same weights at every time step. So it can handle sequences of any length, and something learned at one position (“2009 is a year”) works at every position.

In plain words

  • RNNs specialize in sequences; they scale to much longer sequences than non-specialized networks, and most handle variable lengths.
  • With separate parameters per time index, we could not generalize to unseen lengths, nor share statistical strength across lengths and positions.
  • “I went to Nepal in 2009” vs “In 2009, I went to Nepal”: the year is word 6 or word 2. A fully connected net would learn all language rules separately at each position.
  • 1-D convolution over time also shares parameters, but is shallow: each output depends on a few neighbours. Recurrence produces each output with the same update rule applied to the previous outputs → sharing through a very deep graph.
  • t need not be real time — just the position. RNNs can even run over 2-D images, or backward in time if the whole sequence is observed first.

Terms on this slide

\(\mathbf x^{(t)}\)
The input vector at time step t (t = 1 … τ).
τ (tau)
Sequence length.
Time step
Position in the sequence.
Statistical strength
Evidence pooled from many examples/positions to estimate shared parameters.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Word vectors of size 50, 100 hidden units, sentences of 6 words.
  2. Separate weights per position: 6 × 50 × 100 = 30 000 — and a 7-word sentence has no weights for word 7.
  3. RNN: U (50 × 100) + W (100 × 100) = 15 000, for every length.

10.1 · Unfolding computational graphs

Unfolding: from a loop to a deep chain

A recurrence \(\mathbf h^{(t)}=f(\mathbf h^{(t-1)},\mathbf x^{(t)};\boldsymbol\theta)\) can be unfolded: write out one copy of f per time step. The result is an ordinary (acyclic) computational graph — deep, but with the same θ in every copy.

\[ \mathbf s^{(t)}=f(\mathbf s^{(t-1)};\boldsymbol\theta) \](10.1) \[ \mathbf s^{(3)}=f(\mathbf s^{(2)};\boldsymbol\theta)=f\big(f(\mathbf s^{(1)};\boldsymbol\theta);\boldsymbol\theta\big) \](10.2–10.3) \[ \mathbf h^{(t)}=f(\mathbf h^{(t-1)},\mathbf x^{(t)};\boldsymbol\theta) \](10.4–10.5) \[ \mathbf h^{(t)}=g^{(t)}(\mathbf x^{(t)},\mathbf x^{(t-1)},\dots,\mathbf x^{(1)})=f(\mathbf h^{(t-1)},\mathbf x^{(t)};\boldsymbol\theta) \](10.6–10.7)

In plain words

  • s is the state of a dynamical system; 10.1 is recurrent because s(t) is defined using s(t−1). Unfolding for τ steps removes the recurrence.
  • With an external input x(t), the state contains information about the whole past. When the state is the network’s hidden units we call it h.
  • h(t) is a lossy summary of the past: an arbitrary-length history squeezed into a fixed-length vector. Training decides what to keep (language model: just enough to predict the next words; autoencoder: enough to recover the input).
  • Two drawings: the circuit (compact, with a black square = one-step delay) and the unfolded graph (one node per variable per time step; explicit about computations and information flow).
  • Two advantages: (1) the model always has the same input size (a transition from one state to the next); (2) the same f with the same parameters is used at every step → generalizes to unseen lengths with far fewer training examples.

Terms on this slide

Dynamical system
Something whose state changes over time by a fixed rule.
Unfolding (unrolling)
Mapping the circuit to a graph with one copy per time step.
\(g^{(t)}\)
The function from the whole past input sequence to h(t), factorized into repeated f.
Lossy summary
A compressed description that cannot keep everything.

Calculation, step by step

  1. h(t) = tanh(0.8·h(t−1) + 1·x(t)), h(0) = 0, x = (1, 0, 0).
  2. h(1) = tanh(0 + 1) = 0.762.
  3. h(2) = tanh(0.8 × 0.762 + 0) = tanh(0.609) = 0.544.
  4. h(3) = tanh(0.8 × 0.544) = tanh(0.435) = 0.410 — the memory of x(1) fades.

10.2 · Recurrent neural networks

Three important design patterns

Choose what is recurrent (hidden → hidden, or output → hidden) and where outputs appear (every step, or once at the end). The choice trades power against how easily the network can be trained.

In plain words

  1. Figure 10.3: an output at every step, recurrent connections between hidden units. Very powerful (any function computable by a Turing machine), but expensive to train.
  2. Figure 10.4: an output at every step, recurrence only from the output to the next hidden units. Less powerful — the output must carry all needed information about the past — but trainable in parallel with teacher forcing.
  3. Figure 10.5: recurrent hidden units read the whole sequence, then produce one output (e.g. classify a sentence).

Terms on this slide

U, W, V
Input→hidden, hidden→hidden (or output→hidden), hidden→output weight matrices.
o, L, y
Output, loss and target at a time step.
Sequence-to-one
Many inputs, a single output.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Sentence of τ = 20 words, pattern 10.3: 20 outputs and 20 losses; training must go through all 20 steps sequentially.
  2. Pattern 10.4 with teacher forcing: the 20 losses can be computed at the same time (each uses the true previous output).
  3. Pattern 10.5: 1 output, 1 loss, gradient travels back 20 steps.
10.2 · Forward propagation

Forward propagation and the loss

Start from h(0); for t = 1 … τ apply the same four equations. The total loss is the sum of the per-step losses — for words, the negative log-likelihood of each target given the inputs so far.

\[ \mathbf a^{(t)}=\mathbf b+\mathbf W\mathbf h^{(t-1)}+\mathbf U\mathbf x^{(t)},\qquad \mathbf h^{(t)}=\tanh(\mathbf a^{(t)}) \](10.8–10.9) \[ \mathbf o^{(t)}=\mathbf c+\mathbf V\mathbf h^{(t)},\qquad \hat{\mathbf y}^{(t)}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf o^{(t)}) \](10.10–10.11) \[ L=\sum_tL^{(t)}=-\sum_t\log p_{\text{model}}\big(y^{(t)}\mid\mathbf x^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf x^{(t)}\big) \](10.12–10.14)

In plain words

  • tanh hidden units; discrete outputs (e.g. words): o holds unnormalized log probabilities, softmax turns them into probabilities ŷ.
  • Parameters: biases b, c and matrices U (input→hidden), V (hidden→output), W (hidden→hidden) — the same at every step.
  • p_model(y(t) | …) is read from the entry of ŷ(t) for the correct y(t).
  • Computing the gradient: a forward pass, then a backward pass. Runtime O(τ) that cannot be parallelized (each step needs the previous one), memory O(τ) (all states are stored). This is back-propagation through time (BPTT).

Terms on this slide

a(t)
Pre-activation of the hidden units.
o(t)
Output scores (logits).
ŷ(t)
Predicted probabilities over the vocabulary.
Negative log-likelihood
−log of the probability given to the correct answer.
One-hot
A vector with a single 1 marking a symbol (so Ux picks one column of U).

Calculation, step by step

  1. t = 1, input “a” → U x = (1.0, −0.5); h(0) = 0 → a = (1.0, −0.5).
  2. h = tanh(a) = (0.762, −0.462).
  3. o = V h = (1.224, −1.455, 0.150) → softmax: e^o = (3.40, 0.23, 1.16), sum 4.79 → ŷ = (0.709, 0.049, 0.242).
  4. Target “b”: L(1) = −log 0.049 = 3.02 (the untrained net is wrong).

10.2.1 · Teacher forcing and networks with output recurrence

Teacher forcing

If the only recurrence goes from the output to the next step, maximum likelihood says: during training feed the true output y(t) (not the model’s guess) into step t + 1. The time steps then decouple and can be trained in parallel.

\[ \log p\big(\mathbf y^{(1)},\mathbf y^{(2)}\mid\mathbf x^{(1)},\mathbf x^{(2)}\big) \] \[ =\log p\big(\mathbf y^{(2)}\mid\mathbf y^{(1)},\mathbf x^{(1)},\mathbf x^{(2)}\big)+\log p\big(\mathbf y^{(1)}\mid\mathbf x^{(1)},\mathbf x^{(2)}\big) \](10.15–10.16)

In plain words

  • Removing hidden-to-hidden recurrence (figure 10.4) makes the model strictly less powerful: the outputs, trained to match targets, rarely capture the needed history unless the targets describe the full state.
  • Benefit: for losses comparing prediction and target at each time t, all time steps are decoupled → parallel training, each step’s gradient computed in isolation.
  • At t = 2 the model maximizes the probability of y(2) given the x’s so far and the true y(1) from the training set.
  • Teacher forcing avoids BPTT only when there are no hidden-to-hidden connections; with them, BPTT is still needed (models can use both).
  • Book note: if the network is later used open-loop (its own outputs fed back), the inputs it sees at test time can differ from training — errors can compound (see the demo).

Terms on this slide

Teacher forcing
Training with the ground-truth previous output as the next input.
Output recurrence
Connections from o(t) (or y(t)) to step t + 1.
Open-loop / free running
Generating with the model’s own outputs fed back in.
Conditional maximum likelihood
Maximize p(outputs | inputs) on the training data.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Suppose p(y(1) | x) = 0.8 and p(y(2) | true y(1), x) = 0.5.
  2. log p(y(1), y(2) | x) = log 0.5 + log 0.8 = −0.693 − 0.223 = −0.916.
  3. Each term only needs the true y(1), not the model’s prediction, so both can be computed at once.
10.2.2 · Computing the gradient in an RNN

Back-propagation through time (BPTT)

Just apply ordinary back-propagation to the unrolled graph, from the last time step backward. Each h(t) receives gradient from its own output and from h(t+1); a shared parameter’s gradient is the sum over all time steps.

\[ (\nabla_{\mathbf o^{(t)}}L)_i=\hat y^{(t)}_i-\mathbf 1_{i=y^{(t)}},\qquad \nabla_{\mathbf h^{(\tau)}}L=\mathbf V^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf o^{(\tau)}}L \](10.18–10.19) \[ \nabla_{\mathbf h^{(t)}}L=\mathbf W^{\top}\operatorname{diag}\big(1-(\mathbf h^{(t+1)})^2\big)\,\nabla_{\mathbf h^{(t+1)}}L+\mathbf V^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf o^{(t)}}L \](10.20–10.21) \[ \nabla_{\mathbf c}L=\sum_t\nabla_{\mathbf o^{(t)}}L,\qquad \nabla_{\mathbf b}L=\sum_t\operatorname{diag}\big(1-(\mathbf h^{(t)})^2\big)\nabla_{\mathbf h^{(t)}}L \](10.22–10.23) \[ \nabla_{\mathbf V}L=\sum_t(\nabla_{\mathbf o^{(t)}}L)\,\mathbf h^{(t)\top} \](10.24) \[ \nabla_{\mathbf W}L=\sum_t\operatorname{diag}\big(1-(\mathbf h^{(t)})^2\big)(\nabla_{\mathbf h^{(t)}}L)\,\mathbf h^{(t-1)\top} \](10.25–10.26) \[ \nabla_{\mathbf U}L=\sum_t\operatorname{diag}\big(1-(\mathbf h^{(t)})^2\big)(\nabla_{\mathbf h^{(t)}}L)\,\mathbf x^{(t)\top} \](10.27–10.28)

In plain words

  • No special algorithm: the generalized back-prop of L5 on the unrolled graph; the result works with any gradient-based optimizer.
  • Start at the end: h(τ) only feeds o(τ). Earlier h(t) feed both o(t) and h(t+1) → two terms.
  • \(\operatorname{diag}(1-(\mathbf h^{(t+1)})^2)\) is the Jacobian of tanh at step t + 1.
  • Because W is used at every step, define dummy copies W(t) used only at step t; ∇_W L sums their contributions. x(t) needs no gradient (no parameters above it).

Terms on this slide

BPTT
Back-propagation applied to the graph unrolled through time.
\(\mathbf 1_{i=y}\)
1 for the correct class, 0 otherwise.
diag(v)
A diagonal matrix with the vector v on its diagonal.
W(t)
A dummy copy of W used only at time t, to count each step’s contribution.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Scalar RNN, τ = 2, w = 0.5, u = v = 1, x = (1, 0), loss ½o(2)² at the end only.
  2. Forward: h(1) = tanh(1) = 0.762; h(2) = tanh(0.5 × 0.762) = 0.363; o(2) = 0.363.
  3. Backward: ∂L/∂h(2) = v·o(2) = 0.363; ∂L/∂h(1) = w(1 − h(2)²)·0.363 = 0.5 × 0.868 × 0.363 = 0.158.
  4. ∂L/∂w = (1 − h(2)²)·0.363·h(1) + (1 − h(1)²)·0.158·h(0) = 0.868 × 0.363 × 0.762 + 0 = 0.240.

10.3 · Bidirectional RNNs

Bidirectional RNNs: use the future too

A normal RNN is causal: h(t) only knows the past. When the whole sequence is available, run a second RNN backward from the end, and let each output combine both states.

\[ \mathbf h^{(t)}=f(\mathbf h^{(t-1)},\mathbf x^{(t)}),\qquad \mathbf g^{(t)}=f'(\mathbf g^{(t+1)},\mathbf x^{(t)}) \] \[ \mathbf o^{(t)}=q(\mathbf h^{(t)},\mathbf g^{(t)}) \]

In plain words

  • Speech: the right phoneme now may depend on the next few phonemes (coarticulation) and even the next words.
  • h(t): state of the forward sub-RNN; g(t): state of the backward sub-RNN. o(t) depends on past and future but is most sensitive to inputs around t — no fixed-size window needed.
  • Very successful in handwriting recognition, speech recognition and bioinformatics.
  • 2-D: four RNNs (up, down, left, right). Compared with CNNs, RNNs on images are more expensive but allow long-range lateral interactions within a feature map.

Terms on this slide

Causal
Uses only the past and present, never the future.
g(t)
Hidden state of the RNN moving backward in time.
Coarticulation
Neighbouring sounds change how each sound is pronounced.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Each step keeps a fraction α = 0.7 of the memory.
  2. Forward RNN at t = 8: influence of x(6) ≈ 0.7² = 0.49; of x(10) = 0 (not seen yet).
  3. Bidirectional: x(10) reaches o(8) through g(8): 0.7² = 0.49.
10.5 · Deep recurrent networks

Deep recurrent networks

An RNN has three blocks — input→hidden, hidden→hidden, hidden→output — each normally a single layer. Adding depth helps, but a deeper hidden→hidden path also makes optimization harder; skip connections fix that.

In plain words

  1. (a) Stacked states: lower layers transform the raw input into a more useful representation for higher layers.
  2. (b) A (deep) MLP for each block: allocate enough capacity to each of the three steps. But the shortest path from time t to t + 1 gets longer (one hidden layer in the transition doubles it).
  3. (c) Skip connections in the hidden-to-hidden path restore a short path.

Experiments strongly suggest depth in these operations is advantageous.

Terms on this slide

Transition
The hidden→hidden map from h(t) to h(t+1).
Shortest path
Fewest edges between two variables in the unrolled graph — controls how easily gradients travel.
Skip connection
An edge that jumps over intermediate layers.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Dependency 10 steps back, each edge shrinks the gradient by 0.9.
  2. (a) or (c): 10 edges → 0.9¹⁰ = 0.35. (b): 20 edges → 0.9²⁰ = 0.12.

10.6 · Recursive neural networks

Recursive networks: trees instead of chains

A recursive network applies the same combining function over a tree, not a chain. For a sequence of length τ the depth drops from τ to O(log τ), which may help with long-term dependencies.

\[ \text{depth: chain }=\tau-1\qquad\text{balanced tree }=\lceil\log_2\tau\rceil \]

In plain words

  • Used to process data structures as input to neural nets, in natural language processing and computer vision.
  • Open question: how to structure the tree? (1) A fixed tree independent of the data, e.g. a balanced binary tree; (2) a structure from outside, e.g. a sentence’s parse tree from a language parser; (3) ideally, the learner discovers the appropriate tree for each input.

Terms on this slide

Recursive network
Same weights applied at every internal node of a tree.
Parse tree
The grammatical structure of a sentence as a tree.
⌈·⌉
Round up (ceiling).

  1. τ = 64: chain depth 63, tree depth log₂ 64 = 6.
  2. With 0.8 per level: 0.8⁶³ ≈ 8 × 10⁻⁷ vs 0.8⁶ = 0.26.
10.7 · The challenge of long-term dependencies

Why long-term dependencies are hard

Gradients through many time steps are products of many Jacobians: they usually vanish and occasionally explode. Even when stable, long-term interactions get exponentially smaller weight than short-term ones.

\[ \mathbf h^{(t)}=\mathbf W^{\top}\mathbf h^{(t-1)}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbf h^{(t)}=(\mathbf W^{t})^{\top}\mathbf h^{(0)} \](10.36–10.37) \[ \mathbf W=\mathbf Q\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf Q^{\top}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbf h^{(t)}=\mathbf Q^{\top}\boldsymbol\Lambda^{t}\mathbf Q\,\mathbf h^{(0)} \](10.38–10.39)

In plain words

  • RNNs compose the same function once per step → extremely nonlinear behaviour.
  • Linear, input-free RNN: eigenvalues are raised to the power t. |λ| < 1 decays to zero, |λ| > 1 explodes; components not aligned with the largest eigenvector are eventually discarded.
  • Scalar view: \(w^t\) vanishes or explodes. A non-recurrent net with different random weights per step (variance v) has product variance \(O(v^n)\), so we can pick \(v=\sqrt[n]{v^*}\) — very deep feedforward nets can avoid the problem by careful scaling; RNNs share W, so they cannot.
  • The gradient of long-term interactions is exponentially smaller, so the signal is hidden by short-term fluctuations: SGD’s success probability drops to ~0 for dependencies of only 10–20 steps.

Terms on this slide

Long-term dependency
An output depending on an input many steps earlier.
Eigendecomposition
W = QΛQᵀ: Q orthogonal (eigenvectors), Λ diagonal (eigenvalues).
Vanishing / exploding gradient
Gradients shrinking to ~0 / growing huge over many steps.

Calculation, step by step

  1. W with eigenvalues 0.9 and 1.1, h(0) = 1 along each eigenvector.
  2. After 50 steps: 0.9⁵⁰ = 0.005 and 1.1⁵⁰ = 117.
  3. Feedforward alternative: want product variance v* = 0.01 over n = 10 layers → choose v = 0.01^{1/10} = 0.63 per layer.

10.8 · Echo state networks

Echo state networks: don’t learn the recurrence

Avoid the hard part: fix the input and recurrent weights so the state captures a rich history, and learn only the output weights (linear regression — convex). Set the dynamics near the edge of stability.

\[ \mathbf J^{(t)}=\frac{\partial\mathbf s^{(t)}}{\partial\mathbf s^{(t-1)}},\qquad \text{spectral radius }\rho(\mathbf J)=\max_i|\lambda_i| \]

In plain words

  • Echo state networks (continuous units) and liquid state machines (spiking units) are called reservoir computing.
  • Like kernel machines: map a sequence of any length to a fixed-length vector (the state), then apply a linear predictor.
  • How to set the weights? View the RNN as a dynamical system near the edge of stability: make the eigenvalues of the state-to-state Jacobian close to 1.
  • ESNs fix the weights to a spectral radius such as 3: information is carried forward without exploding because saturating nonlinearities like tanh stabilize it.
  • The same idea is useful for initializing the recurrent weights of a fully trainable RNN.

Terms on this slide

Reservoir
The fixed, random recurrent network.
Readout
The trained linear output layer.
Spectral radius
Largest absolute eigenvalue: how much the map can stretch a vector per step (in the long run).
Convex
Bowl-shaped objective with a single global minimum.

Calculation, step by step

  1. \(\mathbf W=\begin{bmatrix}0.6&0.2\\0.2&0.6\end{bmatrix}\): eigenvalues 0.6 ± 0.2 = 0.8 and 0.4 → ρ = 0.8.
  2. To get ρ = 1.0, scale W by 1/0.8 = 1.25.
  3. Readout: solve the least-squares problem min‖Hw − y‖² — one linear system, no BPTT.

10.9 · Leaky units and other strategies for multiple time scales

Multiple time scales: skips, leaky units, removed connections

Let some parts of the model work on fine time scales (details) and others on coarse ones (carry information from the distant past) — so some paths have derivative products close to 1.

\[ \mu^{(t)}\leftarrow\alpha\,\mu^{(t-1)}+(1-\alpha)\,v^{(t)} \] \[ \text{skip with delay }d:\ \text{gradients shrink like }\lambda^{\tau/d}\ \text{instead of }\lambda^{\tau} \]

In plain words

  1. Skip connections through time: direct edges from t − d to t. Gradients diminish exponentially in τ/d rather than τ (they may still explode, since single-step edges remain).
  2. Leaky units: a linear self-connection with weight α — a running average. α near 1 remembers long, near 0 forgets fast. A real-valued α adjusts the time scale more smoothly than an integer skip length.
  3. Removing connections: replace length-one connections by longer ones; or give groups of leaky units different fixed time scales; or update different groups at different frequencies.

Terms on this slide

Leaky unit
Hidden unit with a linear self-loop (a running average).
Time constant
About 1/(1 − α) steps: how long the unit remembers.
Time scale
How fast a part of the model reacts to change.

Calculation, step by step

  1. α = 0.9, pulse v = 1 at t = 5: μ(5) = 0.1; 10 steps later 0.1 × 0.9¹⁰ = 0.035; time constant 1/0.1 = 10 steps.
  2. τ = 40, d = 4, factor 0.9 per edge: 0.9⁴⁰ = 0.015 without skips vs 0.9¹⁰ = 0.35 with skips.

10.10 · The long short-term memory

LSTM: a leaky unit whose leak is learned and gated

Gated RNNs (LSTM, GRU) are the most effective practical sequence models. The LSTM cell keeps a state with a linear self-loop whose weight (the forget gate) depends on the context — so the network learns when to keep, write, read and forget.

\[ f^{(t)}_i=\sigma\Big(b^f_i+\sum_jU^f_{i,j}x^{(t)}_j+\sum_jW^f_{i,j}h^{(t-1)}_j\Big) \](10.40) forget gate \[ s^{(t)}_i=f^{(t)}_is^{(t-1)}_i+g^{(t)}_i\,\sigma\Big(b_i+\sum_jU_{i,j}x^{(t)}_j+\sum_jW_{i,j}h^{(t-1)}_j\Big) \](10.41) state \[ g^{(t)}_i=\sigma\Big(b^g_i+\sum_jU^g_{i,j}x^{(t)}_j+\sum_jW^g_{i,j}h^{(t-1)}_j\Big) \](10.42) input gate \[ h^{(t)}_i=\tanh\big(s^{(t)}_i\big)\,q^{(t)}_i \](10.43) output \[ q^{(t)}_i=\sigma\Big(b^o_i+\sum_jU^o_{i,j}x^{(t)}_j+\sum_jW^o_{i,j}h^{(t-1)}_j\Big) \](10.44) output gate

In plain words

  • Like leaky units, gated RNNs create paths through time whose derivatives neither vanish nor explode — but the connection weights may change at each time step.
  • Leaky units accumulate evidence; but once used, it may be useful to forget it (e.g. reset at the start of each sub-sequence). The network should learn when.
  • State unit s: the memory, with a linear self-loop. Forget gate f ∈ (0, 1): its weight. Input gate g: how much new input to write. Output gate q: whether to reveal the state.
  • Each cell has the same inputs/outputs as an ordinary unit, plus more parameters and the gating system. Along the state path ∂s(t)/∂s(t−1) = f(t): no squashing, so gradients survive while f ≈ 1.

Terms on this slide

Gate
A sigmoid unit in (0, 1) that multiplies a signal: 0 = block, 1 = pass.
Cell state s
The LSTM’s internal memory.
bᶠ, Uᶠ, Wᶠ
Bias, input weights and recurrent weights of the forget gate (similarly for the other gates).
Internal / outer recurrence
The cell’s self-loop vs. the usual h(t−1) → h(t) recurrence.

Calculation, step by step

  1. s(t−1) = 0.9, f = 0.95, g = 0.002 (input gate closed), candidate 0.5: s(t) = 0.95 × 0.9 + 0.002 × 0.5 = 0.856.
  2. Output gate closed q = 0.0025: h = tanh(0.856) × 0.0025 = 0.0017. Open q = 0.9975: h = 0.694 × 0.9975 = 0.692.
  3. Memory over 9 steps: forget bias 3 → σ(3)⁹ = 0.953⁹ = 0.65; bias 1 → 0.731⁹ = 0.06; bias 0 → 0.5⁹ = 0.002.

10.10.2 · Other gated RNNs

The GRU: one gate to forget and update

In a gated recurrent unit a single update gate u decides both how much to forget and how much to write: copy the old state (u = 1) or replace it by a new target (u = 0). A reset gate r decides which parts of the state are used to compute that target.

\[ h^{(t)}_i=u^{(t-1)}_i\,h^{(t-1)}_i+\big(1-u^{(t-1)}_i\big)\,\tilde h_i \] \[ \tilde h_i=\sigma\Big(b_i+\sum_jU_{i,j}x^{(t-1)}_j+\sum_jW_{i,j}r^{(t-1)}_jh^{(t-1)}_j\Big) \](10.45), h̃ = the “target state” \[ u^{(t)}_i=\sigma\Big(b^u_i+\sum_jU^u_{i,j}x^{(t)}_j+\sum_jW^u_{i,j}h^{(t)}_j\Big) \](10.46) update gate \[ r^{(t)}_i=\sigma\Big(b^r_i+\sum_jU^r_{i,j}x^{(t)}_j+\sum_jW^r_{i,j}h^{(t)}_j\Big) \](10.47) reset gate

In plain words

  • Update gates act like conditional leaky integrators: per dimension, copy it or replace it with the new “target state”.
  • Reset gates control which parts of the state feed the next target → extra nonlinear interaction between past and future state.
  • Many variants are possible (a gate shared by several units; global × local gates). Large studies found no variant clearly beating LSTM and GRU across many tasks.
  • Crucial ingredient: the forget gate. Adding a bias of 1 to the LSTM forget gate makes the LSTM as strong as the best variants.

Calculation, step by step

  1. h(t−1) = 0.6, target h̃ = 0.9, update gate u = 0.8.
  2. h(t) = 0.8 × 0.6 + 0.2 × 0.9 = 0.48 + 0.18 = 0.66.
  3. u = 0: h(t) = 0.9 (replace). u = 1: h(t) = 0.6 (copy).
10.11.1 · Clipping gradients

Clipping gradients

Near a cliff the gradient can be huge, and one step can throw the parameters far into a bad region. Simple fix: if the gradient is too long, shrink it before the update.

\[ \text{if }\ \|\mathbf g\|>v:\qquad \mathbf g\leftarrow\frac{\mathbf g\,v}{\|\mathbf g\|} \](10.48–10.49)

In plain words

  • Two options: clip the minibatch gradient element-wise, or clip its norm ‖g‖ just before the update (v = threshold).
  • Norm clipping rescales all parameters’ gradients jointly with a single factor, so each step is still in the gradient direction; experiments suggest both forms work similarly.
  • Even a random step when the gradient is above the threshold works almost as well. If the gradient is Inf or NaN, a random step of size v usually moves away from the unstable configuration.
  • Clipping helps with exploding gradients, not vanishing ones.

Calculation, step by step

  1. g = (6, 8): ‖g‖ = √(36 + 64) = 10 > v = 5.
  2. Norm clipping: g × 5/10 = (3, 4) — same direction, length 5.
  3. Element-wise to [−5, 5]: (5, 5) — length 7.07 and the direction turns from 53.1° to 45°.

10.11.2 · Regularizing to encourage information flow

Regularizing to encourage information flow

Against vanishing gradients: either build paths with derivative ≈ 1 (LSTM, self-loops, gates), or add a regularizer that asks the back-propagated gradient to keep its size from step to step.

\[ \text{want}\quad\Big\|(\nabla_{\mathbf h^{(t)}}L)\frac{\partial\mathbf h^{(t)}}{\partial\mathbf h^{(t-1)}}\Big\|\ \approx\ \big\|\nabla_{\mathbf h^{(t)}}L\big\| \] \[ \Omega=\sum_t\left(\frac{\big\|(\nabla_{\mathbf h^{(t)}}L)\,\frac{\partial\mathbf h^{(t)}}{\partial\mathbf h^{(t-1)}}\big\|}{\big\|\nabla_{\mathbf h^{(t)}}L\big\|}-1\right)^2 \](10.50)

In plain words

  • Goal: the gradient vector being back-propagated keeps its magnitude, even if the loss only penalizes the output at the end.
  • Computing ∇Ω exactly is hard; approximation: treat the back-propagated vectors ∇_{h(t)}L as constants.
  • Combined with norm clipping it considerably increases the span of dependencies an RNN can learn. Clipping is essential because Ω keeps the dynamics on the edge of explosive gradients.
  • Weakness: not as effective as the LSTM when data is abundant.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Every step multiplies the gradient norm by 0.8, sequence of 30 steps.
  2. Gradient reaching t = 1: 0.8²⁹ = 0.0015 of the original.
  3. Ω = 29 × (0.8 − 1)² = 29 × 0.04 = 1.16; at ratio 1, Ω = 0 and the gradient arrives intact.
Wrap-up

Summary: how it all connects

RNNs = shared parameters through time + a state. Train with BPTT (or teacher forcing). The long chain makes gradients vanish or explode; fix it with short paths (skips, trees), paths with derivative ≈ 1 (leaky units, LSTM/GRU), clipping, or regularization.
