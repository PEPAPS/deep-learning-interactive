8.3.1 · Stochastic gradient descent

SGD and its learning-rate schedule

The minibatch gradient is noisy and that noise doesn’t vanish at the minimum, so SGD must decrease ε over time. Batch gradient descent can keep ε fixed because its gradient really goes to 0.

\[ \hat{\mathbf g}\leftarrow\frac1m\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\sum_iL\big(f(\mathbf x^{(i)};\boldsymbol\theta),y^{(i)}\big) \] \[ \boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta-\epsilon_k\hat{\mathbf g} \]Algorithm 8.1 \[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\epsilon_k=\infty,\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\epsilon_k^2<\infty \](8.12–8.13) sufficient for convergence \[ \epsilon_k=(1-\alpha)\epsilon_0+\alpha\epsilon_\tau,\qquad\alpha=\frac{k}{\tau} \](8.14) then constant ε_τ after k = τ

In plain words

  • The two conditions: steps must add up to infinity (so we can travel anywhere) but their squares must stay finite (so the noise eventually dies out).
  • Practice: decay linearly until iteration τ (a few hundred passes over the data), with \(\epsilon_\tau\approx1\%\) of \(\epsilon_0\).
  • Choose ε₀ by watching learning curves: too large → violent oscillations, cost often increases; too small → slow, may get stuck at high cost. The best ε₀ is usually higher than the one that looks best after the first ~100 iterations.
  • Convergence: excess error \(J(\boldsymbol\theta)-\min J\) is \(O(1/\sqrt k)\) for convex problems and \(O(1/k)\) for strongly convex ones. Batch GD is faster in theory, but generalization error can’t fall faster than O(1/k) anyway (Cramér–Rao), and SGD’s cheap fast start wins on big data.
  • One can also increase the minibatch size during training to blend batch and stochastic behaviour.

Terms on this slide

\(\epsilon_k\)
Learning rate at iteration k.
τ, ε_τ
Iteration where decay stops, and the final learning rate.
Excess error
How much the current cost exceeds the best possible cost.
Strongly convex
Curves upward at least a fixed amount everywhere (a well-shaped bowl).
Learning curve
Plot of the cost against training time.

Calculation, step by step

  1. ε₀ = 0.1, ε_τ = 0.001, τ = 1 000. At k = 500: α = 0.5 → \(\epsilon=0.5(0.1)+0.5(0.001)=0.0505\).
  2. At k = 1 000 and beyond: ε = 0.001.
  3. Check the conditions for \(\epsilon_k=1/k\): \(\sum1/k\) diverges ✓, \(\sum1/k^2=\pi^2/6\approx1.64\) is finite ✓.
  4. A constant ε fails the second condition: \(\sum\epsilon^2=\infty\).

8.3.2 · Momentum

Momentum: keep moving in the consistent direction

Keep a velocity v = decaying average of past negative gradients, and move by v. Back-and-forth components cancel; consistent components add up — up to 1/(1 − α) times faster.

\[ \mathbf v\leftarrow\alpha\mathbf v-\epsilon\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\Big(\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}L\big(f(\mathbf x^{(i)};\boldsymbol\theta),\mathbf y^{(i)}\big)\Big) \] \[ \boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\mathbf v \](8.15–8.16) \[ \text{terminal speed (constant gradient }\mathbf g):\quad\frac{\epsilon\|\mathbf g\|}{1-\alpha} \](8.17)

In plain words

  • Designed for high curvature, small but consistent gradients, or noisy gradients.
  • v is the direction and speed of the parameters (unit mass → velocity = momentum). α ∈ [0, 1) sets how fast old gradients are forgotten; the larger α relative to ε, the more past gradients matter.
  • In a narrow canyon, plain gradient steps bounce across the narrow axis; momentum travels along the canyon.
  • Step size depends on how large and how aligned successive gradients are. α = 0.9 → up to 10× the speed of plain gradient descent.
  • Common α: 0.5, 0.9, 0.99; can be increased over time, but adapting α matters less than shrinking ε.

Terms on this slide

Velocity v
Running, decaying sum of past negative gradients.
α
Momentum coefficient: fraction of the old velocity kept each step.
Terminal velocity
The constant speed reached when the gradient stays the same.

Calculation, step by step

  1. α = 0.9, ε = 0.1, constant gradient g = 1, v₀ = 0.
  2. \(v_1=-0.1\); \(v_2=0.9(-0.1)-0.1=-0.19\); \(v_3=0.9(-0.19)-0.1=-0.271\) …
  3. Limit: \(v=-\epsilon g/(1-\alpha)=-0.1/0.1=-1\) — 10× the plain step of 0.1.
  4. Alternating gradients +1, −1: velocity stays around ±0.05 — oscillations are damped.

8.3.2 · The physics of momentum

Momentum as a ball rolling through syrup

Momentum simulates a particle pushed by the force −∇J(θ) and slowed by viscous drag −v, using Euler steps. Viscous drag is “just right”: turbulent drag never stops the ball, dry friction stops it too early.

\[ \mathbf f(t)=\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\boldsymbol\theta(t) \] \[ \Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbf v(t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol\theta(t),\quad\mathbf f(t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf v(t) \](8.18–8.20) \[ \mathbf f(t)=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}J(\boldsymbol\theta)-c\,\mathbf v(t) \] \[ \xrightarrow{\text{Euler step }\Delta}\quad\mathbf v\leftarrow(1-c\Delta)\mathbf v-\Delta\nabla J,\quad\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\Delta\mathbf v \]

In plain words

  • Newton’s second-order equation is turned into two first-order ones (position and velocity) and solved by Euler’s method: small finite steps in the direction of each derivative → the momentum update.
  • Force 1: \(-\nabla J\) pushes downhill. The ball gathers speed on steep parts and keeps sliding until it starts going uphill.
  • Force 2: \(-\mathbf v\), viscous drag (like syrup), so the ball loses energy and settles in a minimum. Mathematically convenient (integer power 1).
  • Turbulent drag (∝ v², air) is too weak at low speed: the ball never comes to rest. Dry friction (constant, ground) is too strong: it can stop the ball where the gradient is small but non-zero, before the minimum.

Terms on this slide

Euler’s method
Simulate a differential equation by small steps: new = old + Δ × derivative.
Viscous drag
Friction proportional to speed.
Turbulent drag
Friction proportional to speed².
Dry friction
Constant-size friction opposing motion.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Euler with drag c and time step Δ: \(\mathbf v\leftarrow(1-c\Delta)\mathbf v-\Delta\nabla J\).
  2. Match with momentum: α = 1 − cΔ and ε = Δ (absorbing the extra Δ in θ ← θ + Δv into the scale of v).
  3. α = 0.9 ⇒ cΔ = 0.1: each step the syrup removes 10% of the speed.
  4. Dry friction c = 0.5 in the bowl J = ½θ²: the ball stops anywhere with |∇J| = |θ| ≤ 0.5 — up to 0.5 away from the minimum.
8.3.3 · Nesterov momentum

Nesterov momentum: look ahead, then correct

Same as momentum, but the gradient is evaluated after applying the current velocity — at θ + αv. It acts like a correction factor that brakes earlier when the velocity is about to overshoot.

\[ \mathbf v\leftarrow\alpha\mathbf v-\epsilon\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\Big[\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}L\big(f(\mathbf x^{(i)};\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha\mathbf v),\mathbf y^{(i)}\big)\Big] \] \[ \boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\mathbf v \](8.21–8.22)

In plain words

  • α and ε play the same roles as in standard momentum.
  • Standard momentum: gradient at where you are. Nesterov: gradient at where the velocity is about to take you.
  • Convex batch case: the excess error improves from \(O(1/k)\) to \(O(1/k^2)\) after k steps.
  • Stochastic case: no improvement in the convergence rate.
  • Practical note (from the maths of a quadratic): because it reacts to the look-ahead gradient, Nesterov tolerates a slightly smaller maximum ε in very steep directions than standard momentum — that is why the demo uses ε = 0.08.

Terms on this slide

Look-ahead point
θ + αv: where momentum alone would take the parameters.
O(1/k²)
Error shrinks like 1/k²: 10× more steps → 100× less error.

Calculation, step by step

  1. J = ½θ², θ = 2, v = −0.5, α = 0.9, ε = 0.1.
  2. Standard: g = J′(2) = 2 → \(v=0.9(-0.5)-0.1(2)=-0.65\), θ = 1.35.
  3. Nesterov: look ahead to 2 + 0.9(−0.5) = 1.55, g = 1.55 → \(v=-0.45-0.155=-0.605\), θ = 1.395.
  4. Near the minimum the look-ahead gradient is smaller (or already reversed), so Nesterov brakes earlier and overshoots less.
8.5 · Algorithms with adaptive learning rates

One learning rate per parameter

If the directions of sensitivity are roughly axis-aligned, give every parameter its own learning rate and adapt it automatically from the history of its gradients.

In plain words

  • Momentum eases the difficulty of choosing ε, but adds another hyper-parameter. Another idea: per-parameter step sizes.
  • A parameter with consistently large gradients probably sits in a steep direction → smaller steps. One with tiny gradients is in a gentle direction → larger steps.
  • Works best when the important directions line up with the parameter axes (each weight more or less on its own scale). Compare the “axis-aligned valley” and the rotated “stretched bowl” in the demo.
  • Next slides: AdaGrad (sum of all past squared gradients), RMSProp (decaying average), Adam (RMSProp + momentum + bias correction).

Terms on this slide

Adaptive learning rate
A step size that changes automatically during training.
Axis-aligned
The valley’s long and short directions match the parameter axes.
⊙
Element-wise product (each parameter separately).

Calculation, step by step

  1. Loss \(\frac12(0.1x^2+10y^2)\): gradients (0.1x, 10y). Plain GD must use ε < 2/10 = 0.2 → along x each step shrinks error only by \(1-0.2\cdot0.1=0.98\).
  2. An adaptive method divides each gradient by its typical size: steps ≈ ε·sign(g) in both coordinates → both directions progress at a similar rate.
8.5.1 · AdaGrad

AdaGrad: scale by the whole gradient history

Divide each parameter’s step by the square root of the sum of all its past squared gradients. Steep parameters slow down quickly, gentle ones keep moving — but the learning rate can only shrink, sometimes too early.

\[ \mathbf g\leftarrow\frac1m\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\sum_iL\big(f(\mathbf x^{(i)};\boldsymbol\theta),\mathbf y^{(i)}\big),\qquad\mathbf r\leftarrow\mathbf r+\mathbf g\odot\mathbf g \] \[ \Delta\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow-\frac{\epsilon}{\delta+\sqrt{\mathbf r}}\odot\mathbf g,\qquad\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\Delta\boldsymbol\theta\qquad(\delta\approx10^{-7}) \]Algorithm 8.4

In plain words

  • Learning rates scale inversely with \(\sqrt{\sum\text{past }g^2}\) (element-wise). Large partial derivatives → rapidly decreasing rate; small → slowly decreasing.
  • Net effect: more progress in gently sloped directions.
  • Convex optimization: nice theory, converges fast.
  • Deep nets: accumulating squared gradients from the very start can cause a premature and excessive decrease of the effective rate — too small before reaching a locally convex bowl. Works for some models, not all.

Terms on this slide

r
Accumulated squared gradients (one number per parameter).
δ
Tiny constant that avoids division by zero.
Effective learning rate
\(\epsilon/(\delta+\sqrt r)\) for each parameter.

Calculation, step by step

  1. ε = 0.1. Step 1: g = 3 → r = 9 → \(\Delta\theta=-0.1\cdot3/3=-0.1\).
  2. Step 2: g = 4 → r = 9 + 16 = 25 → \(\Delta\theta=-0.1\cdot4/5=-0.08\).
  3. Step 3: g = 0.2 (now in a gentle region) → r = 25.04 → \(\Delta\theta=-0.1\cdot0.2/5.004=-0.004\): the early big gradients still slow it down.
8.5.2 · RMSProp

RMSProp: forget the distant past

Replace AdaGrad’s sum with an exponentially decaying average of squared gradients. After arriving in a convex bowl, RMSProp behaves like AdaGrad started inside that bowl.

\[ \mathbf r\leftarrow\rho\,\mathbf r+(1-\rho)\,\mathbf g\odot\mathbf g \] \[ \Delta\boldsymbol\theta=-\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\delta+\mathbf r}}\odot\mathbf g,\qquad\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\Delta\boldsymbol\theta\qquad(\delta\approx10^{-6}) \]Algorithm 8.5 \[ \text{+ Nesterov: }\ \tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha\mathbf v,\ \ \mathbf g=\nabla J(\tilde{\boldsymbol\theta}),\ \ \mathbf r\leftarrow\rho\mathbf r+(1-\rho)\mathbf g\odot\mathbf g \] \[ \mathbf v\leftarrow\alpha\mathbf v-\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\mathbf r}}\odot\mathbf g,\qquad\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\mathbf v \]Algorithm 8.6

In plain words

  • Performs better than AdaGrad in the non-convex setting.
  • The decaying average discards history from the extreme past, so it can converge rapidly after finding a convex bowl.
  • New hyper-parameter ρ controls the length scale of the average (≈ 1/(1 − ρ) steps).
  • Empirically an effective, practical optimizer for deep networks; can be combined with (Nesterov) momentum.

Terms on this slide

ρ
Decay rate of the moving average (e.g. 0.9).
RMS
Root mean square: \(\sqrt{\text{average of }g^2}\) — the typical gradient size.

Calculation, step by step

  1. ρ = 0.9, ε = 0.1, r₀ = 0. Step 1: g = 3 → r = 0.1·9 = 0.9 → \(\Delta\theta=-0.1\cdot3/\sqrt{0.9}=-0.316\).
  2. Step 2: g = 4 → r = 0.9·0.9 + 0.1·16 = 2.41 → \(\Delta\theta=-0.1\cdot4/1.552=-0.258\).
  3. After ~30 steps of g = 0.2, r ≈ 0.04 + leftover → step ≈ 0.1 · 0.2/0.2 = 0.1: full speed again (AdaGrad would be at 0.004).
8.5.3 · Adam

Adam: adaptive moments

Adam ≈ RMSProp + momentum, with two differences: momentum is an estimate of the gradient’s first moment, and both moment estimates get a bias correction because they start at zero.

\[ t\leftarrow t+1,\quad\mathbf s\leftarrow\rho_1\mathbf s+(1-\rho_1)\mathbf g,\quad\mathbf r\leftarrow\rho_2\mathbf r+(1-\rho_2)\mathbf g\odot\mathbf g \] \[ \hat{\mathbf s}\leftarrow\frac{\mathbf s}{1-\rho_1^t},\quad\hat{\mathbf r}\leftarrow\frac{\mathbf r}{1-\rho_2^t},\quad\Delta\boldsymbol\theta=-\epsilon\frac{\hat{\mathbf s}}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf r}}+\delta},\quad\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\Delta\boldsymbol\theta \]Algorithm 8.7 (defaults ε = 0.001, ρ₁ = 0.9, ρ₂ = 0.999, δ = 10⁻⁸)

In plain words

  • “Adam” = adaptive moments. s estimates the mean of g (first moment); r the uncentered variance (second moment).
  • The straightforward alternative — momentum applied to RMSProp’s rescaled gradients — has no clear theoretical motivation; Adam builds momentum into the moment estimate.
  • Bias correction: s and r start at 0, so early averages are pulled toward 0. RMSProp lacks this correction and its second-moment estimate can be badly biased early on.
  • Generally robust to hyper-parameter choices, though ε sometimes needs changing from the default.

Terms on this slide

First moment
Average of g (the direction of travel).
Second moment
Average of g² (the typical size of g).
Bias correction
Dividing by 1 − ρᵗ undoes the pull toward the zero initial value.
ρ₁, ρ₂
Decay rates of the two moving averages (also written β₁, β₂).

Calculation, step by step

  1. First step, g = 2: s = 0.1·2 = 0.2; r = 0.001·4 = 0.004.
  2. Corrected: ŝ = 0.2/(1 − 0.9) = 2; r̂ = 0.004/(1 − 0.999) = 4.
  3. Step: \(-\epsilon\cdot2/(\sqrt4+10^{-8})=-\epsilon\) — a step of size ε regardless of the gradient’s scale.
  4. Without correction: \(0.2/\sqrt{0.004}=3.16\) → the first step would be 3.16ε.

8.5.4 · Choosing the right optimization algorithm

Which optimizer should you use?

There is no consensus. SGD, SGD + momentum, RMSProp (± momentum), AdaDelta and Adam are all popular; the choice depends largely on how familiar you are with tuning each one.

8.4 · Parameter initialization strategies

Why initialization matters, and breaking symmetry

The starting point decides whether training converges at all, how fast, to what cost — and even how well it generalizes. The one certainty: initial parameters must break symmetry between units.

In plain words

  • Some starting points are so unstable that training fails numerically. Points of similar cost can have very different generalization error.
  • Modern strategies are simple heuristics: we don’t know which nice initial properties survive once learning begins, and good-for-optimization may be bad-for-generalization.
  • Symmetry: two hidden units with the same activation, the same inputs and the same initial parameters get the same updates forever (deterministic algorithm) → redundant.
  • Best: each unit computes a different function, so no input pattern is lost in the null space of forward propagation and no gradient pattern in the null space of back-propagation.
  • Random initialization from a high-entropy distribution in high dimensions is cheap and almost never makes two units identical (explicit orthogonalization, e.g. Gram–Schmidt, costs more).
  • Typically: biases = chosen constants; weights = random Gaussian or uniform (which one hardly matters; the scale matters a lot).

Terms on this slide

Symmetry breaking
Making units start different so they learn different features.
High-entropy distribution
Very spread out / unpredictable (e.g. a broad Gaussian).
Null space
Input directions a layer maps to zero (information lost).

Calculation, step by step

  1. Two units with weights (0.5, 0.5) and the same input x = (1, 0): both compute tanh(0.5) = 0.462.
  2. Output weights equal too ⇒ both receive gradient δ·w·(1 − 0.462²) — identical.
  3. After the update they are still equal; by induction, forever. Random init (e.g. (0.3, −0.7) vs (−0.2, 0.9)) breaks this from step 0.

8.4 · The scale of the initial weights

How large should the initial weights be?

Too small → signals and gradients vanish through many layers. Too large → they explode, units saturate, RNNs become chaotic. Choose the scale so each layer roughly preserves the variance of the signal.

\[ W_{i,j}\sim U\Big(-\frac{1}{\sqrt m},\frac{1}{\sqrt m}\Big) \](8.23) simple rule \[ W_{i,j}\sim U\Big(-\sqrt{\frac{6}{m+n}},\sqrt{\frac{6}{m+n}}\Big) \](8.24) normalized init — m inputs, n outputs

In plain words

  • Larger weights: stronger symmetry breaking, less signal loss through linear parts — but risk exploding values (partly fixable by gradient clipping), saturation and chaos.
  • Optimization view: weights large enough to propagate information. Regularization view: smaller is better; SGD that stays near its start acts like a prior that final θ is near initial θ.
  • The normalized rule compromises between equal activation variance and equal gradient variance across layers.
  • Random orthogonal matrices with a carefully chosen gain for the non-linearity; later, just setting the gain right trained networks 1 000 layers deep. Insight: activations/gradients do a random walk across layers; tuned to preserve norms, they neither vanish nor explode.
  • In practice the best scale is near — not exactly at — the theory: maybe norm preservation isn’t the right criterion; initial properties fade during learning; faster optimization can hurt generalization.
  • \(1/\sqrt m\) rules make every weight tiny in wide layers. Sparse initialization: each unit gets exactly k non-zero weights → total input independent of m, more diversity — but a strong prior that is slow to undo (bad for maxout).
  • Treat the per-layer scale as a hyper-parameter, or look at the std of activations/gradients on a single minibatch (cheap, automatable).

Terms on this slide

Fan-in m / fan-out n
Number of inputs / outputs of a layer.
Gain
Extra multiplier on the init scale chosen for the non-linearity.
Orthogonal matrix
Preserves vector lengths exactly (QᵀQ = I).
Chaos
Extreme sensitivity to tiny input changes, so the forward pass looks random.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Layer with m = 256 inputs, n = 128 outputs. Rule 1: \(U(\pm1/16)=U(\pm0.0625)\).
  2. Normalized: \(\sqrt{6/384}=0.125\) → \(U(\pm0.125)\); variance of U(±a) is a²/3 = 0.0052.
  3. Signal factor per layer ≈ m·Var(w): rule 1 gives \(256\times0.0625^2/3=0.33\) (shrinks 3× per linear layer!); normalized with m = n gives exactly 1.
  4. After 20 layers: \(0.33^{20}\approx10^{-10}\) vs \(1^{20}=1\).

8.4 · Biases and other parameters

Initializing biases (and everything else)

Zero biases are usually fine. Exceptions: output biases that match the data’s marginal statistics, small positive ReLU biases, and gate biases that start “open”. Parameters can also be initialized by learning (pretraining).

\[ \text{softmax}(\mathbf b)=\mathbf c\ \Rightarrow\ b_i=\log c_i\ (+\text{const}) \] \[ p(y\mid\mathbf x)=\mathcal N\big(y\mid\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf x+b,\ 1/\beta\big),\qquad\beta_0=1 \]

In plain words

  1. Output units: with small initial weights the output is set by the bias, so set the bias to the inverse activation of the marginal statistics of the targets (e.g. class frequencies). Same for autoencoders / Boltzmann machines whose outputs should match the input distribution.
  2. Avoid saturation: ReLU hidden bias 0.1 instead of 0. Not compatible with schemes that don’t expect strong bias input (e.g. random-walk init).
  3. Gates: when a unit u ∈ [0, 1] multiplies another unit h (output uh), start with u ≈ 1 so h can learn — e.g. LSTM forget-gate bias = 1.

Variance/precision parameters can safely start at 1. Beyond constants and random values: initialize from an unsupervised model on the same inputs, or from supervised training on a related (even unrelated) task. Some of these help by encoding information about the data; others just set the right scale and make units different.

Terms on this slide

Marginal statistics
Overall averages of the targets, e.g. how often each class occurs.
Gate
A unit in [0, 1] that multiplies another unit, letting it pass or blocking it.
Precision β
1 / variance of a Gaussian output.

Calculation, step by step

  1. Class frequencies c = (0.90, 0.09, 0.01) → b = log c = (−0.105, −2.408, −4.605).
  2. Initial loss with b = 0: \(\log3=1.099\). With b = log c: \(H(\mathbf c)=0.90(0.105)+0.09(2.408)+0.01(4.605)=0.358\).
  3. LSTM forget gate: σ(0) = 0.5 → after 20 steps 0.5²⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁶ of the memory survives; σ(1) = 0.731 → 0.731²⁰ ≈ 0.002.

Wrap-up

Summary: how it all connects

Decay ε for noise; add momentum for speed and stability; use per-parameter rates (RMSProp, Adam) for badly scaled problems; and initialize with random, variance-preserving weights and sensible biases.
